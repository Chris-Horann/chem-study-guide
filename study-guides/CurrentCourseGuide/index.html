Ch. 1; §1.1

Atoms from mass laws

Covered in lecture: Day 1 p.8–15Includes a boxed textbook preview

Lecture: Day 1 p.8–15. Textbook: §1.1, printed pp. 4–7 (PDF 38–41).

Learn

Before anyone could see an atom, careful weighing turned up three patterns: mass is conserved, a compound's composition never changes, and when two elements form more than one compound, the masses come in small whole-number ratios. Dalton's atomic theory explained all three at once. Matter is made of atoms, which are rearranged but never created or destroyed, and which combine in whole numbers.

What we observe

A reaction in a closed container weighs the same before and after. The law was hidden for centuries because escaping gases are hard to weigh (Day 1 p.10).

Water from any source is 11.2% hydrogen by mass (Day 1 p.11).

With 1.01 g of hydrogen, 8.00 g of oxygen makes water, but 16.00 g makes hydrogen peroxide (Day 1 p.13).

What we write

mass % = (mass of element ÷ total mass) × 100%

1.01 ÷ (1.01 + 8.00) × 100% = 11.2% H (Day 1 p.11)

16.00 g ÷ 8.00 g = 2 : 1, a small whole-number ratio (Day 1 p.13)

What the particles are doing

Atoms are rearranged, not destroyed, so the total mass can't change.

Every unit of water has the same atoms, so every sample has the same mass ratio.

H2O2 carries twice as many O atoms per H atom as H2O. Atoms come whole, so the oxygen mass can only jump in whole steps.

Source: Day 1 p.8–14. The particle column is an inference from Dalton's postulates (Day 1 p.14).

Textbook preview (§1.1, PDF p.39–40, printed 5–6)

Laws, hypotheses, and theories

The textbook frames this history as scientific methods in action. A scientific law describes a consistent pattern, like the Law of Constant Composition. A hypothesis is a tentative, testable explanation. A theory is an explanation that has survived extensive testing: Dalton's atomic theory explains why the laws hold. The textbook tells the same story with Proust's two tin oxides, whose O : Sn mass ratios were 0.135 and 0.269, a 1 : 2 ratio (SnO and SnO2).

The lecture covered the laws and Dalton's theory but not the vocabulary of scientific methods.

Recognize it

  • Two compounds of the same elements, “per gram of…” → multiple proportions. Fix one mass first.
  • Before and after masses, gases escaping → conservation of mass.

COAST: a framework for solving problems

Textbook preview: not yet taught in lecture

Textbook previewYour professor hasn't taught this section in Days 1–8. Everything here comes from Gilbert §1.2, printed pp. 7–8 (PDF 41–42), so treat it as reading ahead; its exam status is unknown.

Textbook: §1.2, printed pp. 7–8 (PDF 41–42).

Learn

COAST is the textbook's four-step habit for problems: Collect and Organize, Analyze, Solve, Think about it. It exists to stop the most common failure: grabbing an equation whose symbols look right and plugging numbers in.

What it looks like

Before any arithmetic, a good solver writes down what's given, what's asked, and a rough estimate of the answer.

What you write

Givens with units → a plan connecting them, often worked backward from the answer's units → the calculation → a check against the estimate.

Where the chemistry is

Analyze is where you decide what is physically happening (a photon? an ion pair? a mole of atoms?). That decision picks the relationship.

Source: textbook §1.2, PDF p.41–42 (printed 7–8).

Ch. 1; §1.3

Classes and properties of matter

Textbook preview: not yet taught in lecture

Textbook previewYour professor hasn't taught this section in Days 1–8. Everything here comes from Gilbert §1.3, printed pp. 8–13 (PDF 42–47), so treat it as reading ahead; its exam status is unknown.

Textbook: §1.3, printed pp. 8–13 (PDF 42–47).

Builds on: §1.1 Atoms from mass laws

Learn

Everything with mass that takes up space is matter, and the textbook sorts it with two questions. First: can a physical process separate it? If yes, it's a mixture; if no, it's a pure substance. Second, for a pure substance: can a chemical reaction break it down? If yes, it's a compound; if no, it's an element. Mixtures are homogeneous (uniform throughout) or heterogeneous (distinct regions).

What we observe

Gold melts at 1064 °C and is still gold: a physical property. Iron rusts into a new substance: a chemical property. Oil and water separate into layers (heterogeneous); salt water looks the same everywhere (homogeneous).

What we write

Density, d = m/V (textbook Eq. 1.1), is an intensive property: the same for any amount. Mass and volume are extensive: they grow with the sample.

What the particles are doing

An element's particles are all one kind of atom (single or bonded, like O2). A compound's particles contain two or more kinds of atom in a fixed ratio. A mixture's particles are different substances mingled in any proportion.

Source: textbook §1.3, PDF p.42–47 (printed 8–13); classification flowchart, Fig. 1.2, PDF p.39 (printed 5).

Constant composition (Day 1 p.11, covered in lecture) is what separates a compound from a homogeneous mixture: a compound's composition can't vary.

States of matter

Textbook preview: not yet taught in lecture

Textbook previewYour professor hasn't taught this section in Days 1–8. Everything here comes from Gilbert §1.4, printed pp. 13–16 (PDF 47–50), so treat it as reading ahead; its exam status is unknown.

Textbook: §1.4, printed pp. 13–16 (PDF 47–50).

Builds on: §1.3 Classes and properties of matter

Learn

Solid, liquid, and gas are the same particles with different freedom. In a solid they're locked in an ordered array; in a liquid they touch but slide past each other; in a gas they're far apart and fly freely. Changing state means adding or removing energy.

What we observe

Ice keeps its shape; liquid water takes its container's shape but not its full volume; water vapor fills any container and can be compressed. Dry ice disappears without melting; frost grows on a cold window.

What we write

Solid → liquid (melting), liquid → gas (vaporization), solid → gas (sublimation) absorb energy. The reverse changes (freezing, condensation, deposition) release it (textbook Fig. 1.10).

What the particles are doing

Ice: each H2O held in a hexagonal array, vibrating in place. Liquid: close but tumbling, with neighbors changing. Vapor: widely separated, and the particles themselves take up a negligible fraction of the volume.

Source: textbook §1.4, PDF p.47–50 (printed 13–16); Figs. 1.9–1.10.

Why phase changes absorb or release energy

Particles attract each other. Pulling them apart (melting, vaporizing, subliming) takes energy; letting them come together releases it. That's why the upward arrows in Fig. 1.10 absorb energy and the downward arrows release it. §1.5 adds that particles also gain kinetic energy going from solid to liquid to gas.

Recognize it

  • Name a phase change by its start and end states. The two “skip” changes are sublimation (solid → gas) and deposition (gas → solid).
  • Something cools while a change happens (evaporating sweat, boiling liquid nitrogen) → the change absorbs energy from it.

The spacing idea returns in lecture topics: ionic solids are “dense, highly ordered structures” (crystalline lattices, Day 7 p.16).

Ch. 1; §1.5

Forms of energy

Covered in lecture: Day 1 p.15Includes a boxed textbook preview

Lecture: Day 1 p.15. Textbook: §1.5, printed pp. 16–17 (PDF 50–51).

Builds on: §1.4 States of matter

Learn

Energy is the capacity to do work. It's stored (potential energy: position or composition, like the chemical energy in glucose) or carried as motion (kinetic energy, ½mu²). It moves between objects as work or as heat. Through every change, it is never created or destroyed.

Covered in lecture

Law of Conservation of Energy: “In the course of normal physical and chemical processes, energy cannot be created or destroyed, but it can be converted from one form to another.” Mass–energy: “While the total mass and energy of a system is conserved, mass and energy can interconvert,” E = mc2 (Day 1 p.15).

Source: Day 1 p.15 (“Other Laws we need from Chapter 1”).

Textbook preview (§1.5, PDF p.50–51, printed 16–17)

What we observe

A sprinter burns glucose and speeds up; some energy leaves the body as heat, flowing to the cooler surroundings.

What we write

w = F × d (work = force × distance). KE = ½mu², with mass in kg and speed in m/s, gives joules: 1 J = 1 kg·(m/s)2.

What the particles are doing

Particles have kinetic energy that depends on temperature and state: locked in ice (limited), tumbling in water (more), flying free as vapor (much more).

Conservation as bookkeeping, across the course

The lecture uses conservation of energy without always naming it. In the photoelectric effect, the photon's energy splits into the energy to free the electron (φ) plus the electron's kinetic energy: KE = hν − φ (Day 3 p.19). Mass–energy interconversion matters in nuclear processes, such as the transmutation on Day 3 p.2. In ordinary chemistry, mass is conserved to within any measurable amount; that's what the word “normal” in the slide's wording implies.

  • “Where does the energy go?” → conservation of energy.

Formulas and models

Textbook: §1.6, printed pp. 17–19 (PDF 51–53).

Builds on: §1.1 Atoms from mass laws

Learn

A formula is a count; a model is a picture. A molecular formula says how many atoms of each element are in one molecule. A structural formula shows which atoms are bonded. Ball-and-stick and space-filling models show three-dimensional shape. Ionic compounds have no molecules, so their formulas give only the simplest ratio of ions: an empirical formula.

What we observe

Acetone (a solvent) and acetic acid (vinegar's aroma) are different substances, even though both contain C, H, and O.

What we write

Molecular formulas: C3H6O and C2H4O2. Condensed: CH3COCH3 and CH3COOH. Empirical formula of acetic acid: CH2O.

What the particles are doing

Atoms in a molecule are held by chemical bonds in a characteristic pattern. Seven elements exist as diatomic molecules: H2, N2, O2, F2, Cl2, Br2, I2.

Source: textbook §1.6, PDF p.51–53 (printed 17–19), Figs. 1.14–1.17.

The lecture meets these ideas in Ch. 4: ionic compounds form crystalline lattices, not molecules (Day 7 p.16), and their formulas come from charge balance (Day 7 p.20). Both are covered in lecture; see §4.1 and §4.3.

Measurements and significant figures

Textbook preview: not yet taught in lecture

Textbook previewYour professor hasn't taught this section in Days 1–8. Everything here comes from Gilbert §1.7, printed pp. 19–26 (PDF 53–60), so treat it as reading ahead; its exam status is unknown.

Textbook: §1.7, printed pp. 19–26 (PDF 53–60).

Learn

Every measurement is uncertain, and the digits you write say how uncertain. Significant figures are the certain digits plus one estimated digit. A result can be known only as well as the least well-known value that went into it: the textbook's “weak-link principle.”

What we observe

One balance reads a penny as 2.53 g; a better one reads 2.5271 g. Repeated readings may agree closely (precise) yet all be off from the true mass (not accurate).

What we write

2.53 g has 3 significant figures; 2.5271 g has 5. For 0.0810 g, write 8.10 × 10−2 g: the leading zeros only place the decimal point.

Why it matters here

Counted things (12 eggs, 6 protons) are exact. Measured things (masses, wavelengths) carry uncertainty that propagates through every calculation.

Source: textbook §1.7, PDF p.53–60 (printed 19–26).

Understand

SI units and prefixes

SI base units (Table 1.2): kilogram, meter, kelvin, second, ampere, mole. Prefixes (Table 1.1) scale by powers of 10: k = 103, c = 10−2, m = 10−3, μ = 10−6, n = 10−9, p = 10−12. The joule is 1 kg·(m/s)2.

Which zeros count

RuleExampleSignificant figures
leading zeros never count0.05923
zeros after a decimal point and after a nonzero digit always count3.00 × 1083
trailing zeros without a decimal point are ambiguous; use scientific notation96,500 (9.65 × 104?)3, 4, or 5
zeros between significant digits always count101.34

Calculations: the weak link

× and ÷ → keep the fewest significant figures

(1.52 × 1011 m) ÷ (2.998 × 108 m/s) = 507 s: 3 s.f., set by 1.52.

+ and − → keep the fewest decimal places

124.01 g + 2.5271 g = 126.54 g: hundredths place, set by 124.01.

Round only at the end. Carry extra digits through intermediate steps. For a tie (dropping exactly a 5), the textbook rounds to the nearest even digit: 76.45 → 76.4 and 76.55 → 76.6. Exact numbers (counts, defined equalities like 1 in = 2.54 cm) never limit.

Precision vs. accuracy

Precision is how well repeated measurements agree. Accuracy is how close they are to the true value, checked by calibrating against a known standard. The textbook's dart boards (Fig. 1.22) show all four combinations.

Covered in lecture

The slides list significant figures in italics (Ch. 1 outcome 6, Day 1 p.7) without teaching them, but every worked answer follows the least-precise input: 11.2% (Day 1 p.11), 1.80 × 10−10 m (Day 4 p.12), 1.06 × 10−34 m (Day 4 p.14).

Unit conversions and dimensional analysis

Textbook preview: not yet taught in lecture

Textbook previewYour professor hasn't taught this section in Days 1–8. Everything here comes from Gilbert §1.8, printed pp. 26–31 (PDF 60–65), so treat it as reading ahead; its exam status is unknown.

Textbook: §1.8, printed pp. 26–31 (PDF 60–65).

Builds on: §1.7 Measurements and significant figures

Learn

A conversion factor is a fraction equal to 1, like (1 km / 0.6214 mi). Multiplying by it changes the units but not the quantity. Dimensional analysis means writing every unit and letting them cancel: if the units come out right, the setup is usually right.

What we observe

In 1983 the “Gimli Glider,” a Boeing 767, ran out of fuel mid-flight because its fuel was calculated with a density in lb/L (1.77) where kg/L (0.80) was needed.

What we write

initial units × (desired units / initial units) = desired units (textbook Eq. 1.4). Example: 7700 L × (0.80 kg / 1 L) = 6200 kg.

Why units carry meaning

A unit that doesn't cancel means you're combining quantities that don't belong together. The unit mismatch is a reasoning error you can see.

Source: textbook §1.8, PDF p.60–65 (printed 26–31); Table 1.3, PDF p.54 (printed 20).

Understand

quantity × (new unit ÷ old unit) = quantity in the new unit

Setup The unit you have goes in the denominator, so it cancels.

Chains Multiply several factors in a row: mL → L → qt; km/h → m/s (two factors, one for each unit).

Areas and volumes Square or cube the whole factor: (100 cm / 1 m)3 = 106 cm3/m3.

Significant figures Defined or exact factors (1 in = 2.54 cm; 1 m3 = 1000 L) don't limit them; measured ones, like densities, do.

T(K) = T(°C) + 273.15; T(°C) = (5/9)[T(°F) − 32]

Kelvin and Celsius degrees are the same size, with offset zeros. Fahrenheit degrees are smaller (9 °F = 5 °C), and its zero is offset too, so °F ↔ °C takes two steps. Temperatures aren't converted by a single ratio.

Builds on: §1.7 Measurements and significant figures

Learn

Repeating a measurement answers two questions. How much do the results scatter? That's the standard deviation, a measure of precision. And could the true value plausibly be what we expect? The confidence interval answers that, which judges accuracy. A suspicious point can be tested once, with Grubbs' test, instead of being quietly dropped.

What we observe

A lab checks its method on a control sample of known creatinine concentration (0.681 mg/dL) and gets 0.685, 0.676, 0.669, 0.688, and 0.692 mg/dL.

What we write

x̄ = 0.6820 mg/dL; s = 0.0094 mg/dL; 95% confidence interval: 0.6820 ± 0.0116 mg/dL, which contains 0.681, so the method is accurate.

Where the scatter comes from

Random error, such as dust, vibration, or reading a scale, pushes results up or down equally, giving a bell-shaped (normal) distribution. About 68% of results fall within one s of the mean.

Source: textbook §1.9, PDF p.65–71 (printed 31–37); the creatinine example is the textbook's worked illustration (PDF p.65–67).

Lecture: Day 2 p.31; Day 3 p.7–11. Textbook: §3.2, printed pp. 89–90 (PDF 123–124).

Builds on: §3.1 Light: waves and photons

Learn

Heat an element's vapor and it glows at only a few sharp wavelengths, its emission lines. Shine white light through the cold vapor and those same wavelengths go missing: dark absorption lines. The line pattern is an element's fingerprint. Classical physics could describe it but not explain it.

What we observe

Sunlight through a prism is “NOT continuous, but contained series of very narrow dark lines; the absence of light at specific wavelengths” (Wollaston, Fraunhofer; Day 2 p.31). Flames of elements give “very incomplete spectra with a few lines,” the opposite (Bunsen and Kirchhoff; Day 3 p.8).

What we write

Spectra plotted on a 400–700 nm scale; hydrogen's visible lines at 656, 486, 434, and 410 nm (Day 3 p.9).

What the atoms are doing

Each line is one specific energy change inside the atom. An atom absorbs the same energies it can emit, so the dark and bright lines coincide (Day 3 p.10). Bohr explains why in §3.4.

Source: Day 2 p.31; Day 3 p.7–11.

Kirchhoff's partial explanation: “light is emitted continuously by the sun, but is absorbed by elements in the solar atmosphere” (Day 3 p.10). Ch. 3; §3.3

Quantized energy: Planck and the photoelectric effect

Covered in lecture: Day 3 p.11–19

Lecture: Day 3 p.11–19. Textbook: §3.3, printed pp. 90–95 (PDF 124–129).

Builds on: §3.1 Light: waves and photons, §3.2 Atomic spectra

Learn

Energy comes in packets. Planck proposed it to explain glowing hot objects: light “can't be emitted with ANY arbitrary amount of energy; it's a staircase, not a ramp” (Day 3 p.17). Einstein's photoelectric effect made it undeniable. Red light never ejects electrons from a metal, however bright, but dim violet light does, because one photon either has enough energy or it doesn't.

What we observe

Heated metal glows red, then orange, then white (Day 3 p.12). Violet light on a phototube makes current; dim red makes none; brighter red still makes none (Day 3 p.18).

What we write

E = hν (Day 3 p.17). KE = hν − φ = hν − hν0 (Day 3 p.19): the electron's kinetic energy is the photon's energy minus the threshold energy φ, “also called the ‘work function.’”

What the particles are doing

One photon hands its energy to one electron. If hν is less than φ, the electron stays put, and more photons don't add up. If hν is greater than φ, the leftover energy becomes the electron's motion.

Source: Day 3 p.11–19.

Understand

Blackbody radiation (Day 3 p.12–16)

As a hot object's temperature rises, its emission curve gets higher at every wavelength and its peak shifts to shorter wavelengths: 5000 K peaks near 580 nm, in the visible (Day 3 p.14). Classical theory predicted emission rising without limit at short wavelengths (the black curve on Day 3 p.14–16). Planck's quanta fixed it.

  • Temperature and glow color → the blackbody curve's peak shift.

Ch. 3; §3.4

Hydrogen's spectrum and the Bohr model

Covered in lecture: Day 3 p.21; Day 4 p.3, p.6–10

Lecture: Day 3 p.21; Day 4 p.3, p.6–10. Textbook: §3.4, printed pp. 95–100 (PDF 129–134).

Builds on: §3.2 Atomic spectra, §3.3 Quantized energy: Planck and the photoelectric effect

Learn

Bohr explained hydrogen's lines by quantizing the atom itself: the electron can occupy only certain orbits, each with a fixed energy. When it drops from a higher level to a lower one, the energy lost leaves as a single photon, so each line is one jump.

What we observe

Hydrogen's four visible lines (Day 3 p.9), plus ultraviolet and infrared series. Balmer's empirical formula even predicted new lines, “lines that nobody knew existed in the ultraviolet” (Day 4 p.7).

What we write

Balmer: λ = 364.56 nm × n2/(n2 − 4) (Day 3 p.21). Rydberg: 1/λ = RH(1/n12 − 1/n22) (Day 4 p.7). Bohr: ΔE = −2.178 × 10−18 J (1/nfinal2 − 1/ninitial2) (Day 4 p.10).

What the electron is doing

It sits in level n with energy −2.178 × 10−18 J/n2. Levels crowd together toward n = ∞, where the electron is free. Dropping to n = 1 gives UV (Lyman), to n = 2 visible (Balmer), to n = 3 IR (Paschen).

Source: Day 3 p.21; Day 4 p.3, p.6–10.

Understand

Recognize it

  • Hydrogen (or a one-electron ion) and two n values → ΔE, then λ = hc/|ΔE|.
  • A transition ending on n = 2 in the visible → Balmer's formula also works.
  • A multi-electron atom's spectrum → Bohr does not apply.

Ch. 3; §3.5

Matter waves (de Broglie)

Covered in lecture: Day 4 p.11–17

Lecture: Day 4 p.11–17. Textbook: §3.5, printed pp. 100–103 (PDF 134–137).

Builds on: §3.4 Hydrogen's spectrum and the Bohr model

Learn

If light, a wave, can act like particles, de Broglie reasoned that particles can act like waves. Every moving object has a wavelength λ = h/(mu). It matters only when λ is comparable to the size of the object or its surroundings, which is true for electrons in atoms and never for baseballs.

What we observe

Electron beams make diffraction rings just like X-rays do (Davisson–Germer and G. P. Thomson; Day 4 p.17).

What we write

λ = h/(mu), with m in kg and u in m/s (Day 4 p.11). Electron at 4.05 × 106 m/s: λ = 1.80 × 10−10 m (Day 4 p.12). Baseball, 142 g at 44.0 m/s: λ = 1.06 × 10−34 m (Day 4 p.14).

What the electron is doing

In an atom it's a standing wave. Only orbits that fit a whole number of wavelengths close on themselves, which gives Bohr's n a physical meaning (Day 4 p.15–16).

Source: Day 4 p.11–17.

The string condition counts half-wavelengths; the orbit condition counts whole wavelengths around the circumference. Both are standing-wave conditions.

Recognize it

  • Mass and speed, wavelength asked → λ = h/(mu); convert g → kg first.
  • “Why is the wave nature of a baseball unobservable?” → λ is unimaginably small.

Ch. 3; §3.5

The Heisenberg uncertainty principle

Textbook preview: not yet taught in lecture

Textbook previewYour professor hasn't taught this section in Days 1–8. Everything here comes from Gilbert §3.5, printed pp. 103–104 (PDF 137–138), so treat it as reading ahead; its exam status is unknown.

Textbook: §3.5, printed pp. 103–104 (PDF 137–138).

Builds on: §3.5 Matter waves (de Broglie)

Learn

A wave is spread out, so an electron-wave has no sharp position. Heisenberg's uncertainty principle puts a floor under how well position and momentum can be known together: Δx · mΔu ≥ h/(4π). Pin down where an electron is and its speed becomes uncertain, and vice versa. That's why the electron in an atom is described by probabilities rather than a path.

The thought experiment

To see an electron you'd need γ rays, whose wavelengths are short enough. But γ-ray photons are so energetic that one hit knocks the electron off course.

What we write

Δx · mΔu ≥ h/(4π) (textbook Eq. 3.14). For an electron confined to an atom (Δx ≈ 53 pm), Δu ≥ 1.1 × 106 m/s. For a baseball located to 680 nm, Δu ≥ 5.46 × 10−28 m/s.

What the electron is doing

It has no definite trajectory. The uncertainty is as big as the speed itself, so Bohr's picture of a definite orbit can't be literally right.

Source: textbook §3.5 (The Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle), PDF p.137–138 (printed 103–104).

Ch. 3; §3.6

Wavefunctions, quantum numbers, and Pauli

Covered in lecture: Day 4 p.18; Day 5 p.7–16

Lecture: Day 4 p.18; Day 5 p.7–16. Textbook: §3.6, printed pp. 104–108 (PDF 138–142).

Builds on: §3.4 Hydrogen's spectrum and the Bohr model, §3.5 Matter waves (de Broglie)

Learn

Schrödinger's equation, HΨ = EΨ, treats the electron as a wave. Its solutions, wavefunctions, are the orbitals, each labeled by three quantum numbers: n (size and energy), ℓ (shape), and mℓ (orientation). A fourth, ms (spin), labels the electron. Pauli: no two electrons in an atom share all four, so each orbital holds at most two.

What we observe

A beam of silver atoms through a magnet splits in two, “half … deflected in one direction and half in the other” (Gerlach, 1922; Day 5 p.14).

What we write

n = 1, 2, 3, …; ℓ = 0 … n − 1 (s, p, d, f); mℓ = −ℓ … +ℓ; ms = ±½. Shell n has n2 orbitals: 1, 4, 9, 16 (Table 3.1, Day 5 p.13).

What the electron is doing

|Ψ|2 is the probability density: where the electron is likely to be found. The high-probability regions are the orbitals (Born, 1926; Day 5 p.8). Ψ itself has no physical meaning.

Source: Day 4 p.18; Day 5 p.7–16. The mathematics of the equation is “beyond the scope of this class” (Day 5 p.7).

Ch. 3; §3.7

Orbital shapes and radial distributions

Covered in lecture: Day 5 p.17–20; Day 6 p.12, p.18Includes a boxed textbook preview

Lecture: Day 5 p.17–20; Day 6 p.12, p.18. Textbook: §3.7, printed pp. 108–111 (PDF 142–145).

Builds on: §3.6 Wavefunctions, quantum numbers, and Pauli

Learn

An orbital is a probability cloud. s orbitals are spheres, p orbitals have two lobes along an axis, and most d orbitals have four lobes. To ask how far from the nucleus the electron is likely to be, add up the probability in thin spherical shells: the radial distribution 4πr2ψ2. For 1s it peaks at 53 pm, even though ψ2 itself is largest at the nucleus.

What the pictures show

Dot-density drawings: 1s, 2s, and 3s get bigger with n. 2s and 3s have empty rings where the probability is zero (Day 5 p.18).

What we plot

ψ2 vs. r (density) and 4πr2ψ2 vs. r (distribution) (Day 5 p.17). 1s: one peak at 53 pm. 2s: a small inner peak, a zero near 110 pm, a main peak near 280 pm. 3s: three peaks, with zeros near 100 and 370 pm (Day 5 p.18).

What the electron is doing

Its density is highest right at the nucleus, but a shell there has almost no volume. Farther out the shells are bigger while the density falls, and the product peaks in between.

Source: Day 5 p.17–20; Day 6 p.12, p.18.

Textbook preview (§3.7, PDF p.143–145, printed 109–111)

The textbook names the zero-probability spheres nodes: “The number of nodes in any s orbital is equal to n − 1.” It draws orbitals as boundary surfaces enclosing 90% of the probability. The + and − signs sometimes drawn on p lobes are signs of the wavefunction, not electric charges. It doesn't treat f-orbital shapes, and neither do the slides.

Penetration (Day 6 p.12, p.18)

Compare 2s with 2p: 2s has a small inner lobe near 45 pm that sits inside the 1s core. An electron there feels more of the nuclear charge, which is Coulombic attraction, so 2s is lower in energy than 2p. For n = 3, 3s penetrates most, then 3p, then 3d, so 3s < 3p < 3d in energy. That penetration ordering drives the filling order in §3.8.

In one-electron hydrogen, 2s and 2p have the same energy. Penetration splits subshells only in atoms with several electrons.

  • “Why is 2s lower than 2p?” → penetration.
  • An orbital with two lobes on one axis → p.

Electron configurations

Covered in lecture: Day 6 p.6–20; Day 7 p.6Includes a boxed textbook preview

Lecture: Day 6 p.6–20; Day 7 p.6. Textbook: §3.8, printed pp. 111–119 (PDF 145–153).

Builds on: §3.6 Wavefunctions, quantum numbers, and Pauli, §3.7 Orbital shapes and radial distributions

Learn

An electron configuration lists where all of an atom's electrons are, using three rules. Aufbau: fill the lowest-energy orbitals first. Pauli: at most two electrons per orbital, with opposite spins. Hund: spread electrons across degenerate orbitals before pairing them. The energy order comes from penetration, and it puts 4s below 3d.

What we observe

Elements in a column behave alike; the periodic table's blocks (s, p, d, f) mirror the subshell being filled (Day 6 p.20).

What we write

Notation n ℓ# (Day 6 p.6): Li 1s22s1 = [He]2s1 (Day 6 p.14); C [He]2s22p2 with 2p boxes [↑][↑][ ] (Day 6 p.16); Ni [Ar]4s23d8 in filling order, or [Ar]3d84s2 in n order as written on Day 6 p.21 (both orders appear in lecture).

What the electrons are doing

They repel, so in degenerate orbitals they spread out with parallel spins before pairing (“We will refrain from ‘pairing’ electrons until we HAVE to,” Day 6 p.16). Core electrons shield the valence electrons; the valence electrons do the chemistry (Day 6 p.14).

Source: Day 6 p.6–20; Day 7 p.6.

Ch. 3; §3.9

Configurations of ions

Covered in lecture: Day 6 p.21–23

Lecture: Day 6 p.21–23. Textbook: §3.9, printed pp. 119–122 (PDF 153–156).

Builds on: §3.8 Electron configurations

Learn

To write an ion's configuration, start from the neutral atom. Anions add electrons by the usual rules. Cations lose electrons from the highest principal quantum number first, “regardless of the order in which they were added” (Day 6 p.21). For transition metals, that means 4s empties before 3d.

What we observe

Main-group ions end up with noble-gas configurations: F− and Na+ both equal [Ne] (Day 6 p.21).

What we write

F + e− → F−: [He]2s22p5 → [He]2s22p6 = [Ne]. Na → Na+ + e−: [Ne]3s1 → [Ne]. BUT Ni → Ni2+ + 2e−: [Ar]3d84s2 → [Ar]3d8 (Day 6 p.21).

What the electrons are doing

The highest-n electrons are the outermost and leave first. For Ni that's the two 4s electrons, even though 4s filled before 3d.

Lecture: Day 6 p.24–26; Day 7 p.5, p.7–8. Textbook: §3.10, printed pp. 122–125 (PDF 156–159).

Builds on: §3.8 Electron configurations, §3.9 Configurations of ions

Learn

Atoms get bigger down a group, because each period adds a shell farther out, and smaller across a period, because more protons pull on the same shell. Ions follow from their electrons: a cation loses its outer electrons and shrinks; an anion adds electron–electron repulsion and swells.

What we observe

Radii (pm, Day 6 p.24): Li 152 → Ne 69 across period 2; Li 152 → Cs 265 down group 1. Ions (Day 7 p.8): Na 186 → Na+ 102; Cl 99 → Cl− 181.

What we write

Trends: down a group ↑, across a period ↓; cation < atom < anion; in an isoelectronic series, higher Z → smaller: O2− 140 > F− 133 > Na+ 102 > Mg2+ 72 > Al3+ 54.

What the electrons are doing

Across a period, electrons join the same shell while Zeff rises, pulling the shell in. Down a group, the valence electrons sit in a higher-n shell farther out.

Ionization energy and electron affinity

Covered in lecture: Day 7 p.9–11

Lecture: Day 7 p.9–11. Textbook: §3.11 (IE part), printed pp. 125–128 (PDF 159–162); §3.12, printed pp. 130–133 (PDF 164–167).

Builds on: §3.4 Hydrogen's spectrum and the Bohr model, §3.8 Electron configurations, §3.10 Atomic and ionic size

Learn

Ionization energy (IE) is the energy it takes to pull an electron off a gaseous atom; electron affinity (EA) is the energy change when a gaseous atom gains one. Both track how hard the nucleus pulls on its outer electrons. Successive IEs show a giant jump right after the last valence electron, which is how you count valence electrons from data.

What we observe

IE₁ (kJ/mol, Day 7 p.9): Li 520 → Ne 2081 across period 2; Li 520 → Cs 376 down group 1. Successive IEs of Be: 900, 1758, then 15,050 (Day 7 p.10). EA: Cl −349, F −328, N +7 (circled in red, Day 7 p.11).

What we write

M(g) + hν → M+(g) + e−, IE₁ = +XXX kJ/mol (Day 7 p.9). M(g) + e− → M−(g), EA₁ = ± XXX kJ/mol (Day 7 p.11).

What the electrons are doing

O → O+ removes a paired 2p electron: 2p [↑↓][↑][↑] → [↑][↑][↑] (Day 7 p.9). The big IE jump is where core (1s) electrons begin (the red staircase, Day 7 p.10).

Source: Day 7 p.9–11.

Understand

M(g) + hν → M+(g) + e− IE₁ > 0

Professor's form A photon supplies the energy; the atoms are gaseous; the value is per mole. (The textbook writes Mg → Mg+ + 1 e−, with no photon.)

Trends (the chart's arrows) IE₁ increases across a period and decreases down a group. Printed exceptions: Be 900 > B 801, Mg 738 > Al 578, N 1402 > O 1314, P 1012 > S 1000.

Sign Negative means energy is released: the anion is lower in energy (Cl −349). Positive means energy must be supplied (N +7; the noble gases' calculated values; Be and Mg “>0”).

The textbook's explanations (§3.11–3.12, PDF p.159–164, printed 125–130): Zeff drives both trends; Be > B and Mg > Al because an s electron penetrates better than a p electron; O < N because repulsion within a pair makes the paired electron easier to remove; N's EA is positive because an added electron costs the half-filled 2p3 its stability.

Photoelectron spectroscopy

Textbook preview: not yet taught in lecture

Textbook previewYour professor hasn't taught this section in Days 1–8. Everything here comes from Gilbert §3.11 (PES part), printed pp. 128–130 (PDF 162–164), so treat it as reading ahead; its exam status is unknown.

Textbook: §3.11 (PES part), printed pp. 128–130 (PDF 162–164).

Builds on: §3.11–3.12 Ionization energy and electron affinity, §3.3 Quantized energy: Planck and the photoelectric effect

Learn

Photoelectron spectroscopy (PES) is the photoelectric effect aimed at single atoms, with X-rays strong enough to knock out electrons from every subshell. Each subshell gives one peak. Its position (binding energy) says how tightly those electrons are held; its height says how many electrons there are. The spectrum is direct evidence for electron configurations.

What we observe

Lithium: two peaks, at 6.26 and 0.52 MJ/mol, heights 2 : 1. Aluminum: five peaks, at 151, 12.1, 7.19, 1.09, and 0.58 MJ/mol, heights 2 : 2 : 6 : 2 : 1 (textbook Fig. 3.38, Sample Exercise 3.15).

What we write

binding energy = photon energy − KE of the photoelectron. Li 1s22s1; Al 1s22s22p63s23p1.

What the electrons are doing

Core electrons feel a large Zeff and sit close to the nucleus, so they have large binding energies. Valence electrons are shielded and farther out, so theirs are small. The smallest one equals IE₁.

Source: textbook §3.11 (Photoelectron Spectroscopy), PDF p.162–164 (printed 128–130).

Understand

  • Axis: binding energy decreases from left to right, so inner electrons are on the left and valence electrons on the right.
  • Height: proportional to the number of electrons in the subshell, at most 2 (s), 6 (p), or 10 (d).
  • Across a period: the 1s peak moves to larger binding energy as Z grows (Li 6.26 → Al 151 MJ/mol).
  • Units: MJ/mol; 1 MJ/mol = 1000 kJ/mol, so aluminum's 0.58 MJ/mol 3p peak is 580 kJ/mol, close to its IE₁ of 578 kJ/mol on Day 7 p.9.
Built on lecture ideas

The energy bookkeeping is the photoelectric effect's KE = hν − φ (Day 3 p.19). Subshell capacities come from Pauli (Day 5 p.15), and the orbital order from penetration and shielding (Day 6 p.12–19). The lecture titles §3.11 “Ionization Energies” only; PES itself hasn't been taught.

Binding energies for elements other than Li and Al, used in the explorer, are approximate reference values. Each element's smallest one matches its IE₁ on Day 7 p.9.

The textbook prints lithium's configuration here as “1 2s1” (the 1s2 is missing); it should read 1s22s1.

Ch. 4; §4.1

Bonds, Coulomb energy, and lattices

Covered in lecture: Day 7 p.12–17; Day 8 p.11, p.14–15, p.18Includes a boxed textbook preview

Lecture: Day 7 p.12–17; Day 8 p.11, p.14–15, p.18. Textbook: §4.1, printed pp. 146–151 (PDF 180–185).

Builds on: §3.10 Atomic and ionic size, §3.11–3.12 Ionization energy and electron affinity

Learn

Atoms bond in three ways. Ionic bonds hold oppositely charged ions together by electrostatic attraction. Covalent bonds share electrons between nonmetals. Metallic bonds share mobile electrons among metal atoms. For ions, Coulomb's law makes it quantitative: bigger charges and shorter distances give a more negative (more stable) energy, and a whole crystal lattice is more stable still.

What we observe

Salt (NaCl), hydrogen gas (H2), copper metal (Cu) (Day 7 p.14); white salt crystals, orange-brown bromine, and a spool of copper wire (Day 8 p.15). Ionic compounds form crystalline lattices: “dense, highly ordered structures … almost always solids” (Day 7 p.16).

What we write

Eel = 2.31 × 10−19 J·nm (Q1Q2/d) (Day 7 p.15). Lattice energies U (kJ/mol, Day 7 p.17): NaCl −786, KCl −720, MgO −3791. For a covalent bond: a potential-energy curve with its minimum at −436 kJ/mol and 74 pm for H–H (Day 8 p.11).

What the particles are doing

A cation and an anion attract, and the energy falls as they approach, until their electron clouds overlap and repulsion takes over: the bottom of the well is the bond (Day 7 p.15). Two H atoms do the same, but by sharing their electrons (Day 8 p.11). In a metal, the valence electrons form a “sea” that flows among all the atoms (Day 8 p.14).

Source: Day 7 p.12–17; Day 8 p.11, p.14–15.

Metallic bonds

Electronegativity and polar bonds

Textbook preview: not yet taught in lecture

Textbook previewYour professor hasn't taught this section in Days 1–8. Everything here comes from Gilbert §4.2, printed pp. 151–154 (PDF 185–188), so treat it as reading ahead; its exam status is unknown.

Textbook: §4.2, printed pp. 151–154 (PDF 185–188).

Builds on: §4.1 Bonds, Coulomb energy, and lattices, §3.11–3.12 Ionization energy and electron affinity

Learn

In most covalent bonds the shared pair isn't shared equally: the more electronegative atom pulls it closer and gets a partial negative charge (δ−), leaving the other end δ+. The electronegativity difference Δχ places a bond on a scale from nonpolar covalent through polar covalent to ionic.

What we observe

The HCl molecule has a positive end and a negative end, like a tiny battery: a dipole (textbook Fig. 4.3).

What we write

Hδ+–Clδ−, or an arrow with a + tail pointing toward Cl. Pauling values: H 2.1, Cl 3.0, so Δχ = 0.9 (polar covalent).

What the electrons are doing

In Cl2 the pair sits evenly between identical atoms. In HCl it's pulled toward Cl. In NaCl it's transferred completely, making Na+ and Cl− (textbook Fig. 4.4).

Source: textbook §4.2, PDF p.185–188 (printed 151–154). The Ch. 4 slide lists 4.2 as “Electronegativity and Bonding” (Day 7 p.12; Day 8 p.4), but it hasn't been taught.

Understand

Δχ = |χA − χB|

Guidelines (“more like guidelines than strict limits”): Δχ ≤ 0.4 → essentially nonpolar covalent; 0.4 < Δχ < 2.0 → polar covalent; Δχ ≥ 2.0 → ionic.

δ− goes on the atom with the larger χ.

Examples H–F Δχ = 1.9 (polar covalent, more polar than H–Cl at 0.9); Ca–O Δχ = 2.5 (ionic); Cl–Br Δχ = 0.2 (nonpolar).

Trend and reason

χ increases across a period and decreases down a group, just like IE₁ (textbook Fig. 4.6). Both reflect how strongly the nucleus attracts outer electrons: higher Zeff, smaller atom, stronger pull. The most electronegative elements (F 4.0, O 3.5, N 3.0, Cl 3.0) are at the upper right; the least (Fr, Cs 0.7) are at the lower left.

Connected lecture ideas

The IE₁ trend and Zeff (Day 7 p.5, p.9) and the ionic/covalent definitions (Day 7 p.14). Electronegativity refines the lecture's rule of thumb that metal + nonmetal bonds are ionic and nonmetal + nonmetal bonds are covalent.

Day 8 names electronegativity as one of the “new chemistry” ideas needed when a Lewis structure is hard to draw, along with formal charge and resonance (Day 8 p.26). In this guide it's used again in §4.7 (where negative formal charges go) and §4.9 (which bonds absorb infrared light).

Values are the textbook's Fig. 4.5. It prints Li as 1.1, while some tables give 1.0, so the problems avoid depending on Li.

Ionic formulas and names

Covered in lecture: Day 7 p.18–21; Day 8 p.6

Lecture: Day 7 p.18–21; Day 8 p.6. Textbook: §4.3 (binary ionic, main group), printed pp. 155–156 (PDF 189–190).

Builds on: §3.9 Configurations of ions, §4.1 Bonds, Coulomb energy, and lattices

Learn

An ionic formula records the ratio of ions that makes the total charge zero. Each main-group ion's charge comes from its group: cations lose their valence electrons and anions fill their valence shells, both reaching a noble-gas configuration. The name is just the cation's element name, then the anion's element name ending in “-ide.”

What we observe

Table salt is NaCl; magnesium chloride is MgCl2; the lattice-energy table's compounds (LiF, NaCl, MgO, MgCl2) are circled in red on Day 7 p.21.

What we write

Li+ + Cl− → LiCl; Mg2+ + O2− → MgO; Mg2+ + 2 Cl− → MgCl2 (Day 7 p.20). Names: lithium fluoride, sodium chloride, magnesium oxide, “But also magnesium chloride!” (Day 7 p.21).

What the ions are doing

In the lattice there are no molecules. There are two Cl− ions for every Mg2+, and the formula is that ratio.

Source: Day 7 p.18–21; Day 8 p.6 (the naming slide, repeated).

Understand

“The TOTAL charge has to be zero, so the positive charges and the negative charges have to cancel” (Day 7 p.20)

Steps (1) Find each ion's charge: groups 1, 2, 13 → 1+, 2+, 3+; groups 15, 16, 17 → 3−, 2−, 1− (lose valence electrons, or fill the valence shell; Day 7 p.18–19). (2) Find the smallest numbers of each ion that cancel (the least common multiple of the charges). (3) Write those numbers as subscripts, omitting 1.

Naming Cation = the parent element's name; anion = the parent element's name with its ending changed to -ide (Day 7 p.21). No number prefixes.

The textbook's reason prefixes aren't needed: main-group ions have characteristic charges, so the name fixes the formula; magnesium fluoride can only be MgF2 (textbook §4.3, PDF p.189–190, printed 155–156). The rest of §4.3 follows in the next sections: transition-metal and polyatomic ions and covalent compounds (Day 8), then acids (textbook preview).

Recognize it

  • A metal and a nonmetal → ions, charge balance, a formula with subscripts.
  • A binary ionic name → cation + anion-ide, no “di-.”
  • A given formula like M2O3 → work backward to the metal's charge.

Ch. 4; §4.3

Transition-metal ions and polyatomic ions

Covered in lecture: Day 8 p.7–10Includes a boxed textbook preview

Lecture: Day 8 p.7–10. Textbook: §4.3 (transition metals; polyatomic ions), printed pp. 156–159 (PDF 190–193).

Builds on: §4.3 Ionic formulas and names

Learn

Many transition metals form more than one common ion, and the ions behave differently, so a name has to say which ion is present: a Roman numeral gives the metal ion's charge. Polyatomic ions are small groups of covalently bonded atoms with an overall charge. In formulas and names they act as one unit.

What we observe

Copper and oxygen make two different powders: black CuO and red Cu2O (Day 8 p.7). “These different ions have different chemical properties, so we need to be able to name them differently.”

What we write

CuO, copper (II) oxide; Cu2O, copper (I) oxide (Day 8 p.7). LiNO3, lithium nitrate; NaHCO3, sodium bicarbonate; NH4ClO2, ammonium chlorite (Day 8 p.9).

What the particles are doing

CuO's lattice holds Cu2+ ions; Cu2O's holds Cu+ ions, two for each O2−. A polyatomic ion such as NO3− is “several non-metal atoms covalently bonded together, but with an overall charge” (Day 8 p.8), and it stays intact inside the ionic lattice.

Source: Day 8 p.7–10.

Understand

Roman numeral = the charge on each metal ion

Rule “We add a Roman numeral that represents the charge on the metal to the name of the ion” (Day 8 p.7). The Top Hat question calls this the systematic name (Day 8 p.10).

Formula → name (1) Find the anion's charge from its group (Day 7 p.18–19) or from the polyatomic-ion table. (2) “The TOTAL charge has to be zero” (Day 7 p.20) gives the metal's charge. (3) Write the metal's name, the numeral, then the anion's name. Example: CoCl3: three Cl− carry −3, so the metal is Co3+: cobalt(III) chloride.

Name → formula The numeral gives the cation's charge. Balance it against the anion exactly as on Day 7.

The slides put a space before the numeral, “copper (II) oxide” (Day 8 p.7); the textbook writes “copper(II) oxide” (PDF p.191). Both mean the same, and this guide accepts both. The slide also shows historical names (cupric, cuprous), but “we're not going to worry about them” (Day 8 p.7).

Polyatomic ions

“Polyatomic ions: several non-metal atoms covalently bonded together, but with an overall charge. Almost all of them are anions, but ammonium is a cation” (Day 8 p.8). They're named with the same cation-then-anion rule as binary ionic compounds (Day 8 p.9), and each keeps its own name: NO3− is nitrate, SO42− sulfate, NH4+ ammonium. The full list is in the toolkit.

Lewis symbols and the octet rule

Covered in lecture: Day 8 p.16–18Includes a boxed textbook preview

Lecture: Day 8 p.16–18. Textbook: §4.4 (Lewis symbols; ionic compounds), printed pp. 161–163 (PDF 195–197).

Builds on: §3.8 Electron configurations, §4.1 Bonds, Coulomb energy, and lattices

Learn

G. N. Lewis proposed in 1916 that atoms bond by sharing pairs of electrons until each has a noble gas's valence configuration: eight valence electrons (the octet rule), or two for hydrogen. A Lewis symbol shows an element's valence electrons as dots. Its unpaired dots are the bonds the atom can form, its bonding capacity.

What we observe

Elements form characteristic numbers of bonds. The one the professor asks you to memorize: “carbon ALMOST ALWAYS forms 4 covalent bonds” (Day 8 p.18).

What we write

H, C, N, O, F, and Ne, with dots placed one per side before pairing (Day 8 p.17).

What the electrons are doing

The dots are valence electrons. An unpaired electron can pair with another atom's unpaired electron to make a shared pair, a bond. Paired dots stay on their atom as lone pairs.

Source: Day 8 p.16–18.

Understand

bonding capacity = number of unpaired dots

Octet rule “All main group atoms tend to gain, lose, or share electrons so that each atom has eight valence electrons. Hydrogen only needs two electrons to obtain [He]” (Day 8 p.16).

Lewis symbols “The chemical symbol for an element surrounded by one or more dots representing valence electrons. Write the symbol. Place dots on four sides of the symbol, one at a time before pairing” (Day 8 p.17).

Bonding capacity “The number of covalent bonds an atom forms to have an octet of electrons in its valence shell. The number of unpaired electrons in a Lewis symbol indicates the number of bonds that will form” (Day 8 p.18).

Lewis structures: the five steps

Covered in lecture: Day 8 p.19–26, p.28–30Includes a boxed textbook preview

Lecture: Day 8 p.19–26, p.28–30. Textbook: §4.4 (five steps; double and triple bonds), printed pp. 163–168 (PDF 197–202).

Builds on: §4.4 Lewis symbols and the octet rule

Learn

A Lewis structure shows how a molecule's valence electrons are arranged: shared pairs (bonds, drawn as lines) and unshared lone pairs, placed so that every atom has an octet (H: 2). When single bonds can't give every atom an octet, atoms share two or three pairs: double and triple bonds.

What we observe

Fluorine, oxygen, and nitrogen are all diatomic gases, but their two atoms are held together by one, two, and three shared pairs (Day 8 p.19–20).

What we write

F–F, O=O, N≡N: each line is a shared pair; the dots are lone pairs. “A double bond!” “A triple bond!” (Day 8 p.20).

What the electrons are doing

Each F shares its one unpaired electron. Each O shares two and each N three: “In O2 and N2, more than one pair of electrons are shared in order to achieve a filled valence shell” (Day 8 p.20).

Source: Day 8 p.19–20.

Understand

Five Steps for Drawing Lewis Structures (Day 8 p.21)

  1. Determine the number of valence electrons.
  2. Arrange symbols of elements to show how the atoms are bonded together and connect them with single bonds. The central atom is the one with the largest bonding capacity.
  3. Complete the octet of atoms bonded to each “central” atom by adding lone pairs of electrons (exception: hydrogen only needs two).
  4. Compare the number of valence electrons in the Lewis structure to the number determined in step 1.
  5. Use any leftover electrons to complete the octet on the central atom.

Step 1 as a table Element, number of atoms, valence electrons per atom, total (Day 8 p.22, p.25, p.28).

Covered in lecture: Day 8 p.26–30Includes a boxed textbook preview

Lecture: Day 8 p.26–30. Textbook: §4.5, printed pp. 168–172 (PDF 202–206).

Builds on: §4.4 Lewis structures: the five steps

Learn

Sometimes the five steps give more than one valid Lewis structure, with the same atoms in the same places and the electrons arranged differently. Ozone is the lecture's example: its double bond can go to either end (Day 8 p.30). The textbook calls these resonance structures and says the real molecule is their average (textbook preview, below).

What we observe

Lightning turns ordinary O2 into ozone, O3: “Allotropes: different molecular forms of the same element. O2 vs O3” (Day 8 p.27). The slide's altitude profile shows the ozone layer peaking about 20–25 km up.

What we write

The two ozone structures, shown side by side on Day 8 p.30 (the slide draws no arrow between them).

What the electrons are doing

In either drawing, the central O shares two pairs with one neighbor and one pair with the other. The two drawings differ only in which neighbor gets the second pair.

Source: Day 8 p.26–30.

Understand

Where the two structures come from (Day 8 p.28–30): 18 valence electrons; O–O–O with single bonds; three lone pairs on each end O and the last pair on the center leave the central O with 6 electrons; a lone pair from an end O becomes a second bond. Either end can supply it, so there are two “perfectly valid” structures, the situation the professor flags on Day 8 p.26.

Textbook preview (§4.5 Resonance, PDF p.202–206, printed 168–172; the slides number it §4.6)

Resonance is “the existence of multiple Lewis structures, called resonance structures, which have the same arrangement of atoms but different arrangements of bonding electrons and lone pairs” (PDF p.203).

The real molecule is the average. “Experimental evidence indicates that, technically, neither structure is correct”: both O–O bonds in ozone are 128 pm, about halfway between an O–O single bond (148 pm) and an O=O double bond (121 pm). “It is as though two bonding pairs of electrons connect the center atom to the two others and that a third bonding pair is spread out across all three atoms” (PDF p.203). That spreading is delocalization. It lowers the molecule's energy, which the textbook calls resonance stabilization (PDF p.204).

The arrow: “A double-headed arrow is used between resonance forms to symbolize the averaging effect” (PDF p.204). It's ↔, not the equilibrium arrow ⇌.

Nitrate has three equivalent structures, and the real ion has three identical N–O bonds (Sample Ex. 4.14, PDF p.204–205):

↔↔

The average: each N–O bond is one solid line plus a share of a dashed pair (this guide's drawing convention; the number is the average bond order, 4/3).

The test: look for “one or more atoms having both single and double bonds to two or more atoms of the same element, as in O3 and NO3−” (PDF p.205). Benzene, C6H6, passes it: its ring is drawn as two structures with alternating single and double bonds, or as a hexagon with a circle for the six identical bonds (Fig. 4.10).

A common misreading: that the molecule flips back and forth between the structures. It doesn't. There's one real molecule, and each resonance structure is an imperfect drawing of it.

  • Same atoms, same positions, different electron placement → resonance. Different atom connections → different molecules.
  • Equivalent structures give identical, averaged bonds. Nonequivalent ones need formal charge to rank them (§4.7).

Bond lengths and strengths

Textbook preview: not yet taught in lecture

Textbook previewYour professor hasn't taught this section in Days 1–8. Everything here comes from Gilbert §4.6, printed pp. 172–174 (PDF 206–208), so treat it as reading ahead; its exam status is unknown.

Textbook: §4.6, printed pp. 172–174 (PDF 206–208).

Builds on: §4.5 Resonance, §4.1 Bonds, Coulomb energy, and lattices

Learn

The more electron pairs two atoms share, the shorter and stronger their bond. The number of shared pairs is the bond order. Resonance can make it a fraction, and then the bond's length and strength fall between the whole-number values.

What we observe

Breaking a bond always takes energy. For H–H it's 436 kJ/mol, the depth of the energy well in the lecture's H–H curve (Day 8 p.11). Bonds of higher order take more.

What we write

C–C 154 pm, 348 kJ/mol; C=C 134 pm, 614 kJ/mol; C≡C 120 pm, 839 kJ/mol (Table 4.6). Ozone's bonds: bond order 1.5, 128 pm.

What the electrons are doing

Bond order counts the pairs two atoms share: 1 for a single bond, 2 for a double, 3 for a triple. In ozone, three bonding pairs are spread over two bonds: 1.5 each (PDF p.206).

Source: textbook §4.6, PDF p.206–208 (printed 172–174), Table 4.6 on PDF p.207. The slides list the section (numbered 4.5, “Lengths and Strengths of Covalent Bonds,” bold on Day 8 p.4) but haven't taught it.

Understand

bond order = shared pairs ÷ bonds (averaged over equivalent resonance structures)

bond length
the distance between the bonded nuclei (pm). It decreases as bond order increases.
bond energy
“the energy needed to break one mole of bonds in the gas phase” (kJ/mol). It's always positive, because breaking bonds requires adding energy, and it increases with bond order (PDF p.208).

Averages Table 4.6 values are averages over many compounds, because a bond varies a little with the rest of the molecule: C=O is 799 kJ/mol in CO2 but 743 kJ/mol in formaldehyde (PDF p.208).

Both directions “Bond length, then, can be used to determine bond order, and vice versa” (PDF p.206). Carbonate: 4 bonding pairs over 3 C–O bonds = 4/3 = 1.33, so the length should fall between C–O (143 pm) and C=O (123 pm); measured, 129 pm (Sample Ex. 4.15).

Why more pairs mean shorter, stronger bonds: more shared electrons between the two nuclei attract both nuclei more strongly, pulling them closer and making them harder to separate. Each added pair adds less energy than the one before: C–C → C=C adds 266 kJ/mol, C=C → C≡C adds 225.

Connected lecture ideas

The H–H energy curve (Day 8 p.11) is this section's picture of a single bond: its minimum at 74 pm is the bond length, and its depth, 436 kJ/mol, is the bond energy. The same attraction-then-repulsion shape governs ion pairs (Day 7 p.15).

Recognize it

  • A bond length between Table 4.6's single and double values → a bond order between 1 and 2 (resonance).
  • “Which bond is shorter/stronger?” → compare bond orders first.
  • Bond energy × number of bonds → energy to break all of them (per mole of molecules).

Formal charge

Textbook preview: not yet taught in lecture

Textbook previewYour professor hasn't taught this section in Days 1–8. Everything here comes from Gilbert §4.7, printed pp. 174–178 (PDF 208–212), so treat it as reading ahead; its exam status is unknown.

Textbook: §4.7, printed pp. 174–178 (PDF 208–212).

Builds on: §4.4 Lewis structures: the five steps, §4.5 Resonance, §4.2 Electronegativity and polar bonds

Learn

When several nonequivalent Lewis structures are possible, formal charges help pick the one that best describes the molecule. A formal charge “is not a real charge, but rather an accounting system”: it compares an atom's valence electrons with the electrons assigned to it in one structure, all of its lone-pair electrons plus half of each shared pair.

What we observe

Dinitrogen monoxide, N2O (“laughing gas,” an anesthetic in dentistry), is one substance, but the five steps allow three different Lewis structures (PDF p.208–209).

What we write

ABC

The three structures with their formal charges (PDF p.210).

What the electrons are doing

In this accounting, each atom “owns” its lone pairs and half of every bond it forms. An atom assigned more electrons than it brought as a free atom has a negative formal charge; fewer, a positive one.

Source: textbook §4.7, PDF p.208–212 (printed 174–178). Day 8 p.26 names formal charge as coming chemistry; it hasn't been taught.

Understand

FC = (valence e−) − [(lone-pair e−) + ½(shared e−)] (Eq. 4.2)

valence e−
the free atom's valence electrons: the dots in its Lewis symbol
lone-pair e−
electrons in the atom's lone pairs in this structure (2 per pair)
shared e−
electrons in all the bonds to the atom (2 per bond; a double bond counts 4)

Check The formal charges add up to 0 for a molecule, or to the ion's charge (PDF p.210).

Shortcut If the octet rule is obeyed, an atom with as many bonds as its bonding capacity has FC 0; one more bond gives +1; one fewer gives −1 (PDF p.211).

Choosing the best structure (PDF p.210)

  1. “The best structure is the one in which the formal charge on each atom is zero.”
  2. “If no such structure can be drawn, or if the structure is that of a polyatomic ion, the best structure is the one in which most atoms have formal charges equal to zero or as close to zero as possible.”
  3. “Any negative formal charges should be on the atom(s) of the more/most electronegative element.”

N2O: no structure is all zeros. A (0, +1, −1) and B (−1, +1, 0) tie on criterion 2, and C (−2, +1, +1) is worse. Criterion 3 breaks the tie: in A the −1 sits on O, the more electronegative atom, so A is best. The textbook then adds a reality check: the measured N–N bond is between a triple and a double bond, so the true structure lies between A and B. “Just because a resonance structure scores the best in an FC analysis does not mean that it matches experimental data” (PDF p.210–211).

The textbook calls N2O's three structures resonance structures even though they aren't equivalent; formal charge ranks nonequivalent ones. Equivalent structures, like ozone's two, have matching formal charges and need no ranking.

Connected lecture ideas

The shortcut rests on the lecture's bonding capacity (Day 8 p.18): N forms 3 bonds, O 2, C 4, the halogens 1. Criterion 3 uses electronegativity (§4.2), also named on Day 8 p.26.

  • More than one valid, nonequivalent structure → compute formal charges and apply the criteria.
  • “Formal charge on an atom” → Eq. 4.2, or the bonds-vs.-bonding-capacity shortcut.
  • Formal charges that don't add up to the overall charge → a counting error.

Exceptions to the octet rule

Textbook preview: not yet taught in lecture

Textbook previewYour professor hasn't taught this section in Days 1–8. Everything here comes from Gilbert §4.8, printed pp. 178–183 (PDF 212–217), so treat it as reading ahead; its exam status is unknown.

Textbook: §4.8, printed pp. 178–183 (PDF 212–217).

Builds on: §4.7 Formal charge, §4.4 Lewis structures: the five steps

Learn

The octet rule has three kinds of exceptions. Be, B, and Al form electron-deficient molecules with fewer than 8 electrons around them. Molecules with an odd number of electrons, free radicals, can't pair every electron. And nonmetals in period 3 and below can appear to hold more than an octet.

What we observe

Nitrogen monoxide from car exhaust reacts with O2 to form NO2, a key player in photochemical smog (PDF p.212). Free radicals like these are typically very reactive.

What we write

BeCl2 (Be has 4), NO (N has 7), SF6 (S has 12).

What the electrons are doing

Be has only two valence electrons to share. NO has 5 + 6 = 11 valence electrons, so one must stay unpaired. S in SF6 shares all six of its valence electrons, one with each F.

Source: textbook §4.8, PDF p.212–217 (printed 178–183). The professor has flagged it (“Sometimes it is impossible for every atom to have an octet,” Day 8 p.26) but hasn't taught it.

Ch. 4; §4.9

Vibrating bonds and the greenhouse effect

Textbook preview: not yet taught in lecture

Textbook previewYour professor hasn't taught this section in Days 1–8. Everything here comes from Gilbert §4.9, printed pp. 183–185 (PDF 217–219), so treat it as reading ahead; its exam status is unknown.

Textbook: §4.9, printed pp. 183–185 (PDF 217–219).

Builds on: §4.2 Electronegativity and polar bonds, §3.1 Light: waves and photons

Learn

Covalent bonds “are not rigid. They all vibrate a little, stretching and bending like tiny atomic-sized springs.” A vibration can absorb an infrared photon of the same frequency only if it makes the molecule's charge separation swing back and forth. CO2 has such vibrations and N2 and O2 don't: that's the particle-level root of the greenhouse effect.

What we observe

Earth's surface, at an average of 287 K, emits infrared radiation. CO2 in the atmosphere absorbs some of it and re-emits it in all directions, so part of the heat is sent back down (PDF p.217–218).

What we write

CO2's three vibrations (Fig. 4.13): symmetric stretch, infrared-inactive; asymmetric stretch, infrared-active; bend, infrared-active.

What the particles are doing

Each C=O bond is polar, δ− on O and δ+ on C. Stretching or bending changes the separation of charge, unless two equal changes in opposite directions cancel.

Source: textbook §4.9, PDF p.217–219 (printed 183–185). Not yet taught in lecture.

Understand

IR-active ⇔ the vibration changes the molecule's charge separation

Mechanism “As polar bonds vibrate, the strengths of tiny electrical fields produced by the partial separations of charge in the bonds fluctuate at the same frequencies as their vibrations. The natural frequencies of the vibrations correspond to frequencies of infrared radiation” (PDF p.217). A photon at that frequency can interact with the fluctuating field and be absorbed.

CO2 In the symmetric stretch “the two C=O bonds stretch and then compress at the same time. The two fluctuating electrical fields produced by the two C=O bonds cancel each other out,” so it's infrared-inactive. In the asymmetric stretch, one bond shortens as the other lengthens, and the changes don't cancel; the bend also makes the field fluctuate (PDF p.217–218).

Nonpolar bonds N2 and O2, about 99% of the atmosphere, have bonds between identical atoms. Their stretches create no fluctuating field (the textbook's Concept Test, PDF p.218).

Why that traps heat: after absorbing a photon, a molecule re-emits one of the same energy, and emitted photons “are just as likely to move back toward Earth's surface as they are to go upward toward space” (PDF p.217). Because CO2's active vibrations match the frequencies Earth emits, “carbon dioxide is a potent greenhouse gas” (PDF p.218).

Wien's law puts the peak of a 287 K surface's emission at λmax = (2.898 × 10−3 m·K)/287 K ≈ 10 μm, well into the infrared.

Connected lecture ideas

Infrared light has longer wavelengths and lower photon energies than visible light (E = hν = hc/λ, §3.1). Which bonds are polar comes from electronegativity (§4.2, preview).

Recognize it

  • “Greenhouse gas,” “absorbs infrared” → look for a vibration that changes the charge separation.
  • Two identical atoms (N2, O2, Cl2) → no IR absorption from stretching.
  • A symmetric molecule's symmetric stretch → the changes cancel; an unsymmetric one → they don't.
