Ch. 1; §1.1

Atoms from mass laws

Covered in lecture: Day 1 p.8–15Includes a boxed textbook preview

Lecture: Day 1 p.8–15. Textbook: §1.1, printed pp. 4–7 (PDF 38–41).

Learn

Before anyone could see an atom, careful weighing turned up three patterns: mass is conserved, a compound's composition never changes, and when two elements form more than one compound, the masses come in small whole-number ratios. Dalton's atomic theory explained all three at once. Matter is made of atoms, which are rearranged but never created or destroyed, and which combine in whole numbers.

What we observe

A reaction in a closed container weighs the same before and after. The law was hidden for centuries because escaping gases are hard to weigh (Day 1 p.10).

Water from any source is 11.2% hydrogen by mass (Day 1 p.11).

With 1.01 g of hydrogen, 8.00 g of oxygen makes water, but 16.00 g makes hydrogen peroxide (Day 1 p.13).

What we write

mass % = (mass of element ÷ total mass) × 100%

1.01 ÷ (1.01 + 8.00) × 100% = 11.2% H (Day 1 p.11)

16.00 g ÷ 8.00 g = 2 : 1, a small whole-number ratio (Day 1 p.13)

What the particles are doing

Atoms are rearranged, not destroyed, so the total mass can't change.

Every unit of water has the same atoms, so every sample has the same mass ratio.

H2O2 carries twice as many O atoms per H atom as H2O. Atoms come whole, so the oxygen mass can only jump in whole steps.

Source: Day 1 p.8–14. The particle column is an inference from Dalton's postulates (Day 1 p.14).

Textbook preview (§1.1, PDF p.39–40, printed 5–6)

Laws, hypotheses, and theories

The textbook frames this history as scientific methods in action. A scientific law describes a consistent pattern, like the Law of Constant Composition. A hypothesis is a tentative, testable explanation. A theory is an explanation that has survived extensive testing: Dalton's atomic theory explains why the laws hold. The textbook tells the same story with Proust's two tin oxides, whose O : Sn mass ratios were 0.135 and 0.269, a 1 : 2 ratio (SnO and SnO2).

The lecture covered the laws and Dalton's theory but not the vocabulary of scientific methods.

  • Before and after masses, gases escaping → conservation of mass.

COAST: a framework for solving problems

Textbook preview: not yet taught in lecture

Textbook previewYour professor hasn't taught this section in Days 1–12. Everything here comes from Gilbert §1.2, printed pp. 7–8 (PDF 41–42), so treat it as reading ahead; its exam status is unknown.

Textbook: §1.2, printed pp. 7–8 (PDF 41–42).

Learn

COAST is the textbook's four-step habit for problems: Collect and Organize, Analyze, Solve, Think about it. It exists to stop the most common failure: grabbing an equation whose symbols look right and plugging numbers in.

What it looks like

Before any arithmetic, a good solver writes down what's given, what's asked, and a rough estimate of the answer.

What you write

Givens with units → a plan connecting them, often worked backward from the answer's units → the calculation → a check against the estimate.

Where the chemistry is

Analyze is where you decide what is physically happening (a photon? an ion pair? a mole of atoms?). That decision picks the relationship.

Source: textbook §1.2, PDF p.41–42 (printed 7–8).

Understand

The textbook calls COAST “merely a framework for solving problems, not a recipe.” For simple problems it merges the first two steps. When Think About It fails, go back to Analyze.

Classes and properties of matter

Textbook preview: not yet taught in lecture

Textbook previewYour professor hasn't taught this section in Days 1–12. Everything here comes from Gilbert §1.3, printed pp. 8–13 (PDF 42–47), so treat it as reading ahead; its exam status is unknown.

Textbook: §1.3, printed pp. 8–13 (PDF 42–47).

Builds on: §1.1 Atoms from mass laws

Learn

Everything with mass that takes up space is matter, and the textbook sorts it with two questions. First: can a physical process separate it? If yes, it's a mixture; if no, it's a pure substance. Second, for a pure substance: can a chemical reaction break it down? If yes, it's a compound; if no, it's an element. Mixtures are homogeneous (uniform throughout) or heterogeneous (distinct regions).

What we observe

Gold melts at 1064 °C and is still gold: a physical property. Iron rusts into a new substance: a chemical property. Oil and water separate into layers (heterogeneous); salt water looks the same everywhere (homogeneous).

What we write

Density, d = m/V (textbook Eq. 1.1), is an intensive property: the same for any amount. Mass and volume are extensive: they grow with the sample.

What the particles are doing

An element's particles are all one kind of atom (single or bonded, like O2). A compound's particles contain two or more kinds of atom in a fixed ratio. A mixture's particles are different substances mingled in any proportion.

Source: textbook §1.3, PDF p.42–47 (printed 8–13); classification flowchart, Fig. 1.2, PDF p.39 (printed 5).

States of matter

Textbook preview: not yet taught in lecture

Builds on: §1.3 Classes and properties of matter

Learn

Solid, liquid, and gas are the same particles with different freedom. In a solid they're locked in an ordered array; in a liquid they touch but slide past each other; in a gas they're far apart and fly freely. Changing state means adding or removing energy.

What we observe

Ice keeps its shape; liquid water takes its container's shape but not its full volume; water vapor fills any container and can be compressed. Dry ice disappears without melting; frost grows on a cold window.

What we write

Solid → liquid (melting), liquid → gas (vaporization), solid → gas (sublimation) absorb energy. The reverse changes (freezing, condensation, deposition) release it (textbook Fig. 1.10).

What the particles are doing

Ice: each H2O held in a hexagonal array, vibrating in place. Liquid: close but tumbling, with neighbors changing. Vapor: widely separated, and the particles themselves take up a negligible fraction of the volume.

Source: textbook §1.4, PDF p.47–50 (printed 13–16); Figs. 1.9–1.10.

Why phase changes absorb or release energy

Particles attract each other. Pulling them apart (melting, vaporizing, subliming) takes energy; letting them come together releases it. That's why the upward arrows in Fig. 1.10 absorb energy and the downward arrows release it. §1.5 adds that particles also gain kinetic energy going from solid to liquid to gas.

Recognize it

  • Name a phase change by its start and end states. The two “skip” changes are sublimation (solid → gas) and deposition (gas → solid).
  • Something cools while a change happens (evaporating sweat, boiling liquid nitrogen) → the change absorbs energy from it.

The spacing idea returns in lecture topics: ionic solids are “dense, highly ordered structures” (crystalline lattices, Day 7 p.16).

Ch. 1; §1.5

Forms of energy

Covered in lecture: Day 1 p.15Includes a boxed textbook preview

Lecture: Day 1 p.15. Textbook: §1.5, printed pp. 16–17 (PDF 50–51).

Builds on: §1.4 States of matter

Learn

Energy is the capacity to do work. It's stored (potential energy: position or composition, like the chemical energy in glucose) or carried as motion (kinetic energy, ½mu²). It moves between objects as work or as heat. Through every change, it is never created or destroyed.

Covered in lecture

Law of Conservation of Energy: “In the course of normal physical and chemical processes, energy cannot be created or destroyed, but it can be converted from one form to another.” Mass–energy: “While the total mass and energy of a system is conserved, mass and energy can interconvert,” E = mc2 (Day 1 p.15).

Source: Day 1 p.15 (“Other Laws we need from Chapter 1”).

Textbook preview (§1.5, PDF p.50–51, printed 16–17)

What we observe

A sprinter burns glucose and speeds up; some energy leaves the body as heat, flowing to the cooler surroundings.

What we write

w = F × d (work = force × distance). KE = ½mu², with mass in kg and speed in m/s, gives joules: 1 J = 1 kg·(m/s)2.

What the particles are doing

Particles have kinetic energy that depends on temperature and state: locked in ice (limited), tumbling in water (more), flying free as vapor (much more).

Conservation as bookkeeping, across the course

The lecture uses conservation of energy without always naming it. In the photoelectric effect, the photon's energy splits into the energy to free the electron (φ) plus the electron's kinetic energy: KE = hν − φ (Day 3 p.19). Builds on: §1.1 Atoms from mass laws

Learn

A formula is a count; a model is a picture. A molecular formula says how many atoms of each element are in one molecule. A structural formula shows which atoms are bonded. Ball-and-stick and space-filling models show three-dimensional shape. Ionic compounds have no molecules, so their formulas give only the simplest ratio of ions: an empirical formula.

What we observe

Acetone (a solvent) and acetic acid (vinegar's aroma) are different substances, even though both contain C, H, and O.

What we write

Molecular formulas: C3H6O and C2H4O2. Condensed: CH3COCH3 and CH3COOH. Empirical formula of acetic acid: CH2O.

What the particles are doing

Atoms in a molecule are held by chemical bonds in a characteristic pattern. Seven elements exist as diatomic molecules: H2, N2, O2, F2, Cl2, Br2, I2.

Source: textbook §1.6, PDF p.51–53 (printed 17–19), Figs. 1.14–1.17.

Measurements and significant figures

Textbook preview: not yet taught in lecture

Textbook previewYour professor hasn't taught this section in Days 1–12. Everything here comes from Gilbert §1.7, printed pp. 19–26 (PDF 53–60), so treat it as reading ahead; its exam status is unknown.

Textbook: §1.7, printed pp. 19–26 (PDF 53–60).

Learn

Every measurement is uncertain, and the digits you write say how uncertain. Significant figures are the certain digits plus one estimated digit. A result can be known only as well as the least well-known value that went into it: the textbook's “weak-link principle.”

What we observe

One balance reads a penny as 2.53 g; a better one reads 2.5271 g. Repeated readings may agree closely (precise) yet all be off from the true mass (not accurate).

What we write

2.53 g has 3 significant figures; 2.5271 g has 5. For 0.0810 g, write 8.10 × 10−2 g: the leading zeros only place the decimal point.

Why it matters here

Counted things (12 eggs, 6 protons) are exact. Measured things (masses, wavelengths) carry uncertainty that propagates through every calculation.

Source: textbook §1.7, PDF p.53–60 (printed 19–26).

Understand

SI units and prefixes

SI base units (Table 1.2): kilogram, meter, kelvin, second, ampere, mole. Prefixes (Table 1.1) scale by powers of 10: k = 103, c = 10−2, m = 10−3, μ = 10−6, n = 10−9, p = 10−12. The joule is 1 kg·(m/s)2.

Calculations: the weak link

× and ÷ → keep the fewest significant figures

(1.52 × 1011 m) ÷ (2.998 × 108 m/s) = 507 s: 3 s.f., set by 1.52.

+ and − → keep the fewest decimal places

124.01 g + 2.5271 g = 126.54 g: hundredths place, set by 124.01.

Round only at the end. Carry extra digits through intermediate steps. For a tie (dropping exactly a 5), the textbook rounds to the nearest even digit: 76.45 → 76.4 and 76.55 → 76.6. Exact numbers (counts, defined equalities like 1 in = 2.54 cm) never limit.

Precision vs. accuracy

Builds on: §1.7 Measurements and significant figures

Learn

A conversion factor is a fraction equal to 1, like (1 km / 0.6214 mi). Multiplying by it changes the units but not the quantity. Dimensional analysis means writing every unit and letting them cancel: if the units come out right, the setup is usually right.

What we observe

In 1983 the “Gimli Glider,” a Boeing 767, ran out of fuel mid-flight because its fuel was calculated with a density in lb/L (1.77) where kg/L (0.80) was needed.

What we write

initial units × (desired units / initial units) = desired units (textbook Eq. 1.4). Example: 7700 L × (0.80 kg / 1 L) = 6200 kg.

Why units carry meaning

A unit that doesn't cancel means you're combining quantities that don't belong together. The unit mismatch is a reasoning error you can see.

Source: textbook §1.8, PDF p.60–65 (printed 26–31); Table 1.3, PDF p.54 (printed 20).

Understand

quantity × (new unit ÷ old unit) = quantity in the new unit

Setup The unit you have goes in the denominator, so it cancels.

Chains Multiply several factors in a row: mL → L → qt; km/h → m/s (two factors, one for each unit).

Areas and volumes Square or cube the whole factor: (100 cm / 1 m)3 = 106 cm3/m3.

Builds on: §1.7 Measurements and significant figures

Learn

Repeating a measurement answers two questions. How much do the results scatter? That's the standard deviation, a measure of precision. And could the true value plausibly be what we expect? The confidence interval answers that, which judges accuracy. A suspicious point can be tested once, with Grubbs' test, instead of being quietly dropped.

What we observe

A lab checks its method on a control sample of known creatinine concentration (0.681 mg/dL) and gets 0.685, 0.676, 0.669, 0.688, and 0.692 mg/dL.

What we write

x̄ = 0.6820 mg/dL; s = 0.0094 mg/dL; 95% confidence interval: 0.6820 ± 0.0116 mg/dL, which contains 0.681, so the method is accurate.

Where the scatter comes from

Random error, such as dust, vibration, or reading a scale, pushes results up or down equally, giving a bell-shaped (normal) distribution. About 68% of results fall within one s of the mean.

Source: textbook §1.9, PDF p.65–71 (printed 31–37); the creatinine example is the textbook's worked illustration (PDF p.65–67).

Learn

Heat an element's vapor and it glows at only a few sharp wavelengths, its emission lines. Shine white light through the cold vapor and those same wavelengths go missing: dark absorption lines. The line pattern is an element's fingerprint. Classical physics could describe it but not explain it.

What we observe

Spectra plotted on a 400–700 nm scale; hydrogen's visible lines at 656, 486, 434, and 410 nm (Day 3 p.9).

Each line is one specific energy change inside the atom. An atom absorbs the same energies it can emit, so the dark and bright lines coincide (Day 3 p.10). Bohr explains why in §3.4.

Ch. 3; §3.3

Quantized energy: Planck and the photoelectric effect

Covered in lecture: Day 3 p.11–19

Lecture: Day 3 p.11–19. Textbook: §3.3, printed pp. 90–95 (PDF 124–129).

Builds on: §3.1 Light: waves and photons, §3.2 Atomic spectra

Learn

Energy comes in packets. Planck proposed it to explain glowing hot objects: light “can't be emitted with ANY arbitrary amount of energy; it's a staircase, not a ramp” (Day 3 p.17). Einstein's photoelectric effect made it undeniable. Red light never ejects electrons from a metal, however bright, but dim violet light does, because one photon either has enough energy or it doesn't.

What we observe

Heated metal glows red, then orange, then white (Day 3 p.12). Violet light on a phototube makes current; dim red makes none; brighter red still makes none (Day 3 p.18).

What we write

E = hν (Day 3 p.17). KE = hν − φ = hν − hν0 (Day 3 p.19): the electron's kinetic energy is the photon's energy minus the threshold energy φ, “also called the ‘work function.’”

What the particles are doing

One photon hands its energy to one electron. If hν is less than φ, the electron stays put, and more photons don't add up. If hν is greater than φ, the leftover energy becomes the electron's motion.

Source: Day 3 p.11–19.

Understand

Blackbody radiation (Day 3 p.12–16)

As a hot object's temperature rises, its emission curve gets higher at every wavelength and its peak shifts to shorter wavelengths: 5000 K peaks near 580 nm, in the visible (Day 3 p.14). Classical theory predicted emission rising without limit at short wavelengths (the black curve on Day 3 p.14–16). Planck's quanta fixed it.

Hydrogen's spectrum and the Bohr model

Covered in lecture: Day 3 p.21; Day 4 p.3, p.6–10

Lecture: Day 3 p.21; Day 4 p.3, p.6–10. Textbook: §3.4, printed pp. 95–100 (PDF 129–134).

Builds on: §3.2 Atomic spectra, §3.3 Quantized energy: Planck and the photoelectric effect

Learn

Bohr explained hydrogen's lines by quantizing the atom itself: the electron can occupy only certain orbits, each with a fixed energy. When it drops from a higher level to a lower one, the energy lost leaves as a single photon, so each line is one jump.

What we observe

Hydrogen's four visible lines (Day 3 p.9), plus ultraviolet and infrared series. Balmer's empirical formula even predicted new lines, “lines that nobody knew existed in the ultraviolet” (Day 4 p.7).

What we write

Balmer: λ = 364.56 nm × n2/(n2 − 4) (Day 3 p.21). Rydberg: 1/λ = RH(1/n12 − 1/n22) (Day 4 p.7). Bohr: ΔE = −2.178 × 10−18 J (1/nfinal2 − 1/ninitial2) (Day 4 p.10).

What the electron is doing

It sits in level n with energy −2.178 × 10−18 J/n2. Levels crowd together toward n = ∞, where the electron is free. Dropping to n = 1 gives UV (Lyman), to n = 2 visible (Balmer), to n = 3 IR (Paschen).

Source: Day 3 p.21; Day 4 p.3, p.6–10.

Understand

Matter waves (de Broglie)

Covered in lecture: Day 4 p.11–17

Lecture: Day 4 p.11–17. Textbook: §3.5, printed pp. 100–103 (PDF 134–137).

Builds on: §3.4 Hydrogen's spectrum and the Bohr model

Learn

If light, a wave, can act like particles, de Broglie reasoned that particles can act like waves. Every moving object has a wavelength λ = h/(mu). It matters only when λ is comparable to the size of the object or its surroundings, which is true for electrons in atoms and never for baseballs.

What we observe

Electron beams make diffraction rings just like X-rays do (Davisson–Germer and G. P. Thomson; Day 4 p.17).

What we write

λ = h/(mu), with m in kg and u in m/s (Day 4 p.11). Electron at 4.05 × 106 m/s: λ = 1.80 × 10−10 m (Day 4 p.12). Baseball, 142 g at 44.0 m/s: λ = 1.06 × 10−34 m (Day 4 p.14).

What the electron is doing

In an atom it's a standing wave. Only orbits that fit a whole number of wavelengths close on themselves, which gives Bohr's n a physical meaning (Day 4 p.15–16).

Source: Day 4 p.11–17.

Ch. 3; §3.5

The Heisenberg uncertainty principle

Textbook preview: not yet taught in lecture

Textbook previewYour professor hasn't taught this section in Days 1–12. Everything here comes from Gilbert §3.5, printed pp. 103–104 (PDF 137–138), so treat it as reading ahead; its exam status is unknown.

Textbook: §3.5, printed pp. 103–104 (PDF 137–138).

Builds on: §3.5 Matter waves (de Broglie)

Learn

A wave is spread out, so an electron-wave has no sharp position. Heisenberg's uncertainty principle puts a floor under how well position and momentum can be known together: Δx · mΔu ≥ h/(4π). Pin down where an electron is and its speed becomes uncertain, and vice versa. That's why the electron in an atom is described by probabilities rather than a path.

The thought experiment

To see an electron you'd need γ rays, whose wavelengths are short enough. But γ-ray photons are so energetic that one hit knocks the electron off course.

What we write

Δx · mΔu ≥ h/(4π) (textbook Eq. 3.14). For an electron confined to an atom (Δx ≈ 53 pm), Δu ≥ 1.1 × 106 m/s. For a baseball located to 680 nm, Δu ≥ 5.46 × 10−28 m/s.

What the electron is doing

It has no definite trajectory. The uncertainty is as big as the speed itself, so Bohr's picture of a definite orbit can't be literally right.

Source: textbook §3.5 (The Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle), PDF p.137–138 (printed 103–104).

Wavefunctions, quantum numbers, and Pauli

Covered in lecture: Day 4 p.18; Day 5 p.7–16

Lecture: Day 4 p.18; Day 5 p.7–16. Textbook: §3.6, printed pp. 104–108 (PDF 138–142).

Builds on: §3.4 Hydrogen's spectrum and the Bohr model, §3.5 Matter waves (de Broglie)

Learn

Schrödinger's equation, HΨ = EΨ, treats the electron as a wave. Its solutions, wavefunctions, are the orbitals, each labeled by three quantum numbers: n (size and energy), ℓ (shape), and mℓ (orientation). A fourth, ms (spin), labels the electron. Pauli: no two electrons in an atom share all four, so each orbital holds at most two.

What we observe

A beam of silver atoms through a magnet splits in two, “half … deflected in one direction and half in the other” (Gerlach, 1922; Day 5 p.14).

What we write

n = 1, 2, 3, …; ℓ = 0 … n − 1 (s, p, d, f); mℓ = −ℓ … +ℓ; ms = ±½. Shell n has n2 orbitals: 1, 4, 9, 16 (Table 3.1, Day 5 p.13).

What the electron is doing

|Ψ|2 is the probability density: where the electron is likely to be found. The high-probability regions are the orbitals (Born, 1926; Day 5 p.8). Ψ itself has no physical meaning.

Source: Day 4 p.18; Day 5 p.7–16. The mathematics of the equation is “beyond the scope of this class” (Day 5 p.7).

Orbital shapes and radial distributions

Covered in lecture: Day 5 p.17–20; Day 6 p.12, p.18Includes a boxed textbook preview

Lecture: Day 5 p.17–20; Day 6 p.12, p.18. Textbook: §3.7, printed pp. 108–111 (PDF 142–145).

Builds on: §3.6 Wavefunctions, quantum numbers, and Pauli

Learn

An orbital is a probability cloud. s orbitals are spheres, p orbitals have two lobes along an axis, and most d orbitals have four lobes. To ask how far from the nucleus the electron is likely to be, add up the probability in thin spherical shells: the radial distribution 4πr2ψ2. For 1s it peaks at 53 pm, even though ψ2 itself is largest at the nucleus.

What the pictures show

Dot-density drawings: 1s, 2s, and 3s get bigger with n. 2s and 3s have empty rings where the probability is zero (Day 5 p.18).

What we plot

ψ2 vs. r (density) and 4πr2ψ2 vs. r (distribution) (Day 5 p.17). 1s: one peak at 53 pm. 2s: a small inner peak, a zero near 110 pm, a main peak near 280 pm. 3s: three peaks, with zeros near 100 and 370 pm (Day 5 p.18).

What the electron is doing

Its density is highest right at the nucleus, but a shell there has almost no volume. Farther out the shells are bigger while the density falls, and the product peaks in between.

Source: Day 5 p.17–20; Day 6 p.12, p.18.

Textbook preview (§3.7, PDF p.143–145, printed 109–111)

Ionization energy and electron affinity

Learn

Ionization energy (IE) is the energy it takes to pull an electron off a gaseous atom; electron affinity (EA) is the energy change when a gaseous atom gains one. Textbook preview: not yet taught in lecture

Textbook previewYour professor hasn't taught this section in Days 1–12. Everything here comes from Gilbert §3.11 (PES part), printed pp. 128–130 (PDF 162–164), so treat it as reading ahead; its exam status is unknown.

Textbook: §3.11 (PES part), printed pp. 128–130 (PDF 162–164).

Builds on: §3.11–3.12 Ionization energy and electron affinity, §3.3 Quantized energy: Planck and the photoelectric effect

Learn

Photoelectron spectroscopy (PES) is the photoelectric effect aimed at single atoms, with X-rays strong enough to knock out electrons from every subshell. Each subshell gives one peak. Its position (binding energy) says how tightly those electrons are held; its height says how many electrons there are. The spectrum is direct evidence for electron configurations.

What we observe

Lithium: two peaks, at 6.26 and 0.52 MJ/mol, heights 2 : 1. Aluminum: five peaks, at 151, 12.1, 7.19, 1.09, and 0.58 MJ/mol, heights 2 : 2 : 6 : 2 : 1 (textbook Fig. 3.38, Sample Exercise 3.15).

What we write

binding energy = photon energy − KE of the photoelectron. Li 1s22s1; Al 1s22s22p63s23p1.

What the electrons are doing

Core electrons feel a large Zeff and sit close to the nucleus, so they have large binding energies. Valence electrons are shielded and farther out, so theirs are small. The smallest one equals IE₁.

Source: textbook §3.11 (Photoelectron Spectroscopy), PDF p.162–164 (printed 128–130).

Understand

  • Axis: binding energy decreases from left to right, so inner electrons are on the left and valence electrons on the right.
  • Height: proportional to the number of electrons in the subshell, at most 2 (s), 6 (p), or 10 (d).
  • Across a period: the 1s peak moves to larger binding energy as Z grows (Li 6.26 → Al 151 MJ/mol).
  • Units: MJ/mol; 1 MJ/mol = 1000 kJ/mol, so aluminum's 0.58 MJ/mol 3p peak is 580 kJ/mol, close to its IE₁ of 578 kJ/mol on Day 7 p.9.

Bonds, Coulomb energy, and lattices

Covered in lecture: Day 7 p.12–17; Day 8 p.11, p.14–15, p.18Includes a boxed textbook preview

Lecture: Day 7 p.12–17; Day 8 p.11, p.14–15, p.18. Textbook: §4.1, printed pp. 146–151 (PDF 180–185).

Builds on: §3.10 Atomic and ionic size, §3.11–3.12 Ionization energy and electron affinity

Learn

Atoms bond in three ways. Ionic bonds hold oppositely charged ions together by electrostatic attraction. Covalent bonds share electrons between nonmetals. Metallic bonds share mobile electrons among metal atoms. For ions, Coulomb's law makes it quantitative: bigger charges and shorter distances give a more negative (more stable) energy, and a whole crystal lattice is more stable still.

What we observe

Salt (NaCl), hydrogen gas (H2), copper metal (Cu) (Day 7 p.14); white salt crystals, orange-brown bromine, and a spool of copper wire (Day 8 p.15). Ionic compounds form crystalline lattices: “dense, highly ordered structures … almost always solids” (Day 7 p.16).

What we write

Eel = 2.31 × 10−19 J·nm (Q1Q2/d) (Day 7 p.15). Lattice energies U (kJ/mol, Day 7 p.17): NaCl −786, KCl −720, MgO −3791. For a covalent bond: a potential-energy curve with its minimum at −436 kJ/mol and 74 pm for H–H (Day 8 p.11).

What the particles are doing

A cation and an anion attract, and the energy falls as they approach, until their electron clouds overlap and repulsion takes over: the bottom of the well is the bond (Day 7 p.15). Two H atoms do the same, but by sharing their electrons (Day 8 p.11). In a metal, the valence electrons form a “sea” that flows among all the atoms (Day 8 p.14).

Source: Day 7 p.12–17; Day 8 p.11, p.14–15.

Ionic formulas and names

Covered in lecture: Day 7 p.18–21; Day 8 p.6

Lecture: Day 7 p.18–21; Day 8 p.6. Textbook: §4.3 (binary ionic, main group), printed pp. 155–156 (PDF 189–190).

Builds on: §3.9 Configurations of ions, §4.1 Bonds, Coulomb energy, and lattices

Learn

An ionic formula records the ratio of ions that makes the total charge zero. Each main-group ion's charge comes from its group: cations lose their valence electrons and anions fill their valence shells, both reaching a noble-gas configuration. The name is just the cation's element name, then the anion's element name ending in “-ide.”

What we observe

Table salt is NaCl; magnesium chloride is MgCl2; the lattice-energy table's compounds (LiF, NaCl, MgO, MgCl2) are circled in red on Day 7 p.21.

What we write

Li+ + Cl− → LiCl; Mg2+ + O2− → MgO; Mg2+ + 2 Cl− → MgCl2 (Day 7 p.20). Names: lithium fluoride, sodium chloride, magnesium oxide, “But also magnesium chloride!” (Day 7 p.21).

What the ions are doing

In the lattice there are no molecules. There are two Cl− ions for every Mg2+, and the formula is that ratio.

Source: Day 7 p.18–21; Day 8 p.6 (the naming slide, repeated).

Understand

“The TOTAL charge has to be zero, so the positive charges and the negative charges have to cancel” (Day 7 p.20)

Steps (1) Find each ion's charge: groups 1, 2, 13 → 1+, 2+, 3+; groups 15, 16, 17 → 3−, 2−, 1− (lose valence electrons, or fill the valence shell; Day 7 p.18–19). (2) Find the smallest numbers of each ion that cancel (the least common multiple of the charges). (3) Write those numbers as subscripts, omitting 1.

Naming Cation = the parent element's name; anion = the parent element's name with its ending changed to -ide (Day 7 p.21). No number prefixes.

The textbook's reason prefixes aren't needed: main-group ions have characteristic charges, so the name fixes the formula; magnesium fluoride can only be MgF2 (textbook §4.3, PDF p.189–190, printed 155–156). The rest of §4.3 follows in the next sections: transition-metal and polyatomic ions and covalent compounds (Day 8), then acids (textbook preview).

Recognize it

  • A metal and a nonmetal → ions, charge balance, a formula with subscripts.

Transition-metal ions and polyatomic ions

Covered in lecture: Day 8 p.7–10Includes a boxed textbook preview

Lecture: Day 8 p.7–10. Textbook: §4.3 (transition metals; polyatomic ions), printed pp. 156–159 (PDF 190–193).

Builds on: §4.3 Ionic formulas and names

Learn

Many transition metals form more than one common ion, and the ions behave differently, so a name has to say which ion is present: a Roman numeral gives the metal ion's charge. Polyatomic ions are small groups of covalently bonded atoms with an overall charge. In formulas and names they act as one unit.

What we observe

Copper and oxygen make two different powders: black CuO and red Cu2O (Day 8 p.7). “These different ions have different chemical properties, so we need to be able to name them differently.”

What we write

CuO, copper (II) oxide; Cu2O, copper (I) oxide (Day 8 p.7). LiNO3, lithium nitrate; NaHCO3, sodium bicarbonate; NH4ClO2, ammonium chlorite (Day 8 p.9).

What the particles are doing

CuO's lattice holds Cu2+ ions; Cu2O's holds Cu+ ions, two for each O2−. A polyatomic ion such as NO3− is “several non-metal atoms covalently bonded together, but with an overall charge” (Day 8 p.8), and it stays intact inside the ionic lattice.

Source: Day 8 p.7–10.

Understand

Roman numeral = the charge on each metal ion

Rule “We add a Roman numeral that represents the charge on the metal to the name of the ion” (Day 8 p.7). The Top Hat question calls this the systematic name (Day 8 p.10).

Formula → name (1) Find the anion's charge from its group (Day 7 p.18–19) or from the polyatomic-ion table. (2) “The TOTAL charge has to be zero” (Day 7 p.20) gives the metal's charge. (3) Write the metal's name, the numeral, then the anion's name. Example: CoCl3: three Cl− carry −3, so the metal is Co3+: cobalt(III) chloride.

Name → formula The numeral gives the cation's charge. Balance it against the anion exactly as on Day 7.

The slides put a space before the numeral, “copper (II) oxide” (Day 8 p.7); the textbook writes “copper(II) oxide” (PDF p.191). Both mean the same, and this guide accepts both. The slide also shows historical names (cupric, cuprous), but “we're not going to worry about them” (Day 8 p.7).

Polyatomic ions

“Polyatomic ions: several non-metal atoms covalently bonded together, but with an overall charge. Almost all of them are anions, but ammonium is a cation” (Day 8 p.8). They're named with the same cation-then-anion rule as binary ionic compounds (Day 8 p.9), and each keeps its own name: NO3− is nitrate, SO42− sulfate, NH4+ ammonium. The full list is in the toolkit.

Exam note“You will be provided with a table like this on the exams, so you don't need to memorize them… but the sooner you learn the ones we see frequently, the faster you'll be able to solve whatever problem is being asked” (Day 8 p.8).
Textbook preview (§4.3, PDF p.190–193, printed 156–159)

No numeral for one-ion metals: the textbook uses Roman numerals “unless the metal forms just one cation, as with Ag+, Cd2+, and Zn2+” (PDF p.191). ZnO is zinc oxide.

Parentheses: when a formula needs two or more of the same polyatomic ion, the ion goes in parentheses before its subscript: magnesium phosphate is Mg3(PO4)2 (Sample Ex. 4.6, PDF p.193). The lecture's examples were all 1 : 1 and needed none.

-ate and -ite: “The -ate oxoanion of an element has more oxygen atoms than its -ite counterpart” (sulfate SO42−, sulfite SO32−). With four oxoanions, per-…-ate has the most O and hypo-…-ite the fewest: perchlorate, chlorate, chlorite, hypochlorite (PDF p.192, Table 4.5).

  • Look-alike: covalent compounds use number prefixes, not numerals (the next section).

Two nonmetals can combine in more than one ratio: sulfur and oxygen form both SO2 and SO3. There are no ion charges to fix the ratio, so a covalent name has to state how many atoms of each element the molecule contains. Greek number prefixes do that.

The same two elements make different compounds: SO2 and SO3 are two different substances, and sulfur and fluorine form S2F2 (Day 8 p.13).

What we write

SO2, sulfur dioxide; SO3, sulfur trioxide; S2F2, disulfur difluoride (Day 8 p.13).

What the particles are doing

A covalent compound is made of molecules: atoms held together by shared electrons (Day 7 p.14). The prefixes count the atoms of each element in one molecule.

Lewis symbols and the octet rule

Covered in lecture: Day 8 p.16–18Includes a boxed textbook preview

Lecture: Day 8 p.16–18. Textbook: §4.4 (Lewis symbols; ionic compounds), printed pp. 161–163 (PDF 195–197).

Builds on: §3.8 Electron configurations, §4.1 Bonds, Coulomb energy, and lattices

Learn

G. N. Lewis proposed in 1916 that atoms bond by sharing pairs of electrons until each has a noble gas's valence configuration: eight valence electrons (the octet rule), or two for hydrogen. A Lewis symbol shows an element's valence electrons as dots. Its unpaired dots are the bonds the atom can form, its bonding capacity.

What we observe

Elements form characteristic numbers of bonds. The one the professor asks you to memorize: “carbon ALMOST ALWAYS forms 4 covalent bonds” (Day 8 p.18).

What we write

H, C, N, O, F, and Ne, with dots placed one per side before pairing (Day 8 p.17).

What the electrons are doing

The dots are valence electrons. An unpaired electron can pair with another atom's unpaired electron to make a shared pair, a bond. Paired dots stay on their atom as lone pairs.

Source: Day 8 p.16–18.

Understand

bonding capacity = number of unpaired dots

Octet rule “All main group atoms tend to gain, lose, or share electrons so that each atom has eight valence electrons. Hydrogen only needs two electrons to obtain [He]” (Day 8 p.16).

Lewis symbols “The chemical symbol for an element surrounded by one or more dots representing valence electrons. Write the symbol. Place dots on four sides of the symbol, one at a time before pairing” (Day 8 p.17).

Bonding capacity “The number of covalent bonds an atom forms to have an octet of electrons in its valence shell. The number of unpaired electrons in a Lewis symbol indicates the number of bonds that will form” (Day 8 p.18).

Lewis structures: the five steps

Covered in lecture: Day 8 p.19–26, p.28–30; Day 9 p.6, p.19–20Includes a boxed textbook preview

Lecture: Day 8 p.19–26, p.28–30; Day 9 p.6, p.19–20. Textbook: §4.4 (five steps; double and triple bonds), printed pp. 163–168 (PDF 197–202).

Builds on: §4.4 Lewis symbols and the octet rule

Learn

A Lewis structure shows how a molecule's valence electrons are arranged: shared pairs (bonds, drawn as lines) and unshared lone pairs, placed so that every atom has an octet (H: 2). When single bonds can't give every atom an octet, atoms share two or three pairs: double and triple bonds.

What we observe

Fluorine, oxygen, and nitrogen are all diatomic gases, but their two atoms are held together by one, two, and three shared pairs (Day 8 p.19–20).

What we write

F–F, O=O, N≡N: each line is a shared pair; the dots are lone pairs. “A double bond!” “A triple bond!” (Day 8 p.20).

What the electrons are doing

Each F shares its one unpaired electron. Each O shares two and each N three: “In O2 and N2, more than one pair of electrons are shared in order to achieve a filled valence shell” (Day 8 p.20).

Source: Day 8 p.19–20.

Understand

Five Steps for Drawing Lewis Structures (Day 8 p.21)

  1. Determine the number of valence electrons.
  2. Arrange symbols of elements to show how the atoms are bonded together and connect them with single bonds. The central atom is the one with the largest bonding capacity.
  3. Complete the octet of atoms bonded to each “central” atom by adding lone pairs of electrons (exception: hydrogen only needs two).
  4. Compare the number of valence electrons in the Lewis structure to the number determined in step 1.
  5. Use any leftover electrons to complete the octet on the central atom.

Step 1 as a table Element, number of atoms, valence electrons per atom, total (Day 8 p.22, p.25, p.28).

Ammonia, NH3 (Day 8 p.22–23): N 1 × 5 = 5, H 3 × 1 = 3, total 8. N goes in the center (bonding capacity 3); three N–H bonds use 6 electrons; the last 2 go on N as a lone pair.

Ozone, O3 (Day 8 p.28–30): 3 × 6 = 18. After the single bonds, three lone pairs on each end O, and the last pair on the center, the central O has only 6 electrons (p.29). A lone pair on an end O becomes a second bond, and it can be either end, so the professor shows two structures (p.30): Day 9 repeats this exercise to open resonance (Day 9 p.6; §4.5).

Dinitrogen monoxide, N2O (Day 9 p.19–20): N 2 × 5 = 10, O 1 × 6 = 6, “TOTAL: 16”; skeleton N–N–O. After the single bonds and the end atoms' lone pairs (step 3), the central N has only 4 electrons. Lone pairs on the end atoms can become shared pairs in three different ways, and each result gives every atom an octet: “These structures are not equivalent!” (Day 9 p.21). Choosing among them takes formal charge (§4.7).

The five steps stop at step 5. When the central atom is still short, the slides' ethyne and ozone examples show the move: turn lone pairs on a neighboring atom into shared pairs, making a multiple bond (Day 8 p.25, p.30). The textbook writes this into its own step 5 (PDF p.197).

“Further Considerations: … Sometimes it is impossible for every atom to have an octet. Sometimes it is possible to draw more than one perfectly valid Lewis structure. We'll need to learn some new chemistry to help us resolve these situations: Electronegativity, Formal Charge, Resonance” (Day 8 p.26). Day 9 taught all of them: electronegativity (§4.2), resonance (§4.5), formal charge (§4.7), and the species where an octet is impossible or exceeded (§4.8).

A Lewis structure is flat: it shows which atoms are connected, not the molecule's 3-D shape. Methane's Lewis structure is a flat cross, but its bond angles are 109.5° (Day 10 p.7; textbook PDF p.196, p.202). Shape is the next step, and it starts here: “to know the electron-pair geometry, you must have a valid Lewis structure!” (Day 10 p.8; §5.1–5.2).

Resonance

Covered in lecture: Day 8 p.26–30; Day 9 p.2, p.6–13Includes a boxed textbook preview

Lecture: Day 8 p.26–30; Day 9 p.2, p.6–13. Textbook: §4.5, printed pp. 168–172 (PDF 202–206).

Builds on: §4.4 Lewis structures: the five steps

Learn

When two or more valid Lewis structures “can be interconverted by just moving electrons, we say they are ‘in resonance’” (Day 9 p.8). Ozone is the lecture's example, and its real bonds match neither drawing: “neither structure alone is correct.” The molecule is “ALWAYS somewhere in between. An average of the two” (Day 9 p.9).

What we observe

Both O–O bonds in ozone are 128 pm long. A typical O–O bond is 148 pm and a typical O=O bond 121 pm: “The bonds in ozone are somewhere in between” (Day 9 p.7). Ozone itself is an allotrope of oxygen, made from O2 by lightning (Day 8 p.27).

What we write

↔

The two structures joined by the resonance arrow ↔ (Day 9 p.9). They are “equivalent. That does not mean they are the same” (Day 9 p.8).

What the electrons are doing

Turning one drawing into the other moves only electrons, never atoms: the red curved arrows on Day 9 p.9 show which pairs shift. In the real molecule those electrons are “delocalized,” spread over both bonds at once (Day 9 p.10).

Source: Day 9 p.6–13, with Day 8 p.26–30.

Understand

From the Day 8 answer to the hybrid (Day 9 p.6–10)

  1. Two answers. The five steps give O=O–O and O–O=O: the Day 8 practice exercise, repeated (p.6).
  2. “Which of these structures is correct? Interestingly, the answer turns out to be ‘neither.’” Both bonds are 128 pm, between O–O (148 pm, drawn as H–O–O–H) and O=O (121 pm, drawn as O2) (p.7).
  3. Equivalent, not the same. “We can transform one of these structures into the other by only moving electrons,” so they are “in resonance,” and each is a “resonance structure” (p.8).
  4. Curved arrows and ↔. Red curved arrows show which pairs move, with a plain → to the result; ↔ joins the resonance structures. “The molecule is NOT ‘changing’ back and forth between these two structures” (p.9).
  5. The resonance hybrid. “An even better way to draw this structure is as the ‘resonance hybrid’, where we use dashed lines to indicate partial bonds” (p.10).

Lecture: Day 9 p.19–26. Textbook: §4.7, printed pp. 174–178 (PDF 208–212).

Builds on: §4.4 Lewis structures: the five steps, §4.5 Resonance, §4.2 Electronegativity and polar bonds

Learn

Sometimes the five steps give several valid structures that are “not equivalent.” Which is right? “Well, again, the answer is ‘none of them’… But one of them is better than the other two, and closer to the real structure” (Day 9 p.21). To choose, the professor introduces formal charge, “a comparison of how many electrons the atoms have in the compound to how many they had as free atoms” (Day 9 p.22).

What we observe

Dinitrogen monoxide, N2O, is one substance with one real structure. The practice exercise on Day 9 p.19 counts its valence electrons in a table: N 2 × 5 = 10, O 1 × 6 = 6, “TOTAL: 16,” with the skeleton N–N–O.

What we write

ABC

The three structures the five steps allow (Day 9 p.20): each uses all 16 electrons and gives every atom an octet.

What the electrons are doing

In this bookkeeping each atom “owns” its lone pairs and half of every bond it forms. An atom assigned more electrons than it brought as a free atom gets a negative formal charge; fewer, a positive one.

Source: Day 9 p.19–26.

Understand

FC = (valence e−) − [(unshared e−) + ½(e− in bonding pairs)]

“For each atom” (Day 9 p.22): 1. Determine the number of valence electrons in the free atom. 2. Count the number of electrons in lone pairs on the atom in the structure. 3. Count the number of electrons in bonds to the atom and divide that number by 2. 4. Sum the results of 2 and 3 and subtract from the number determined in step 1.

valence e−
the free atom's valence electrons: the dots in its Lewis symbol (blue on the slide)
unshared e−
electrons in the atom's lone pairs in this structure, 2 per pair, plus any single unpaired electron (red)
e− in bonding pairs
all the electrons in bonds to the atom, 2 per bond, so a double bond counts 4 (green)

Worked: structure A (:N≡N–O with three lone pairs on O). End N: 5 − (2 + ½ × 6) = 0. Central N: 5 − (0 + ½ × 8) = +1. O: 6 − (6 + ½ × 2) = −1. Sum 0, as it must be for a molecule.

Choosing among structures (Day 9 p.23)

“When choosing between multiple Lewis structures:”

  1. “The best structure is the one in which the formal charge on each atom is zero.”
  2. “If no such structure can be drawn, the best structure is the one where most of the atoms have formal charges equal to zero or as close to zero as possible.”
  3. “Any negative formal charges should be on the atom(s) of the more/most electronegative element.”
  4. “If you've done it right, the sum of the formal charges will be the charge on the molecule/ion.”

Vibrating bonds and the greenhouse effect

Textbook preview: not yet taught in lecture

Textbook previewYour professor hasn't taught this section in Days 1–12. Everything here comes from Gilbert §4.9, printed pp. 183–185 (PDF 217–219), so treat it as reading ahead; its exam status is unknown.

Textbook: §4.9, printed pp. 183–185 (PDF 217–219).

Builds on: §4.2 Electronegativity and polar bonds, §3.1 Light: waves and photons

Learn

Covalent bonds “are not rigid. They all vibrate a little, stretching and bending like tiny atomic-sized springs.” A vibration can absorb an infrared photon of the same frequency only if it makes the molecule's charge separation swing back and forth. CO2 has such vibrations and N2 and O2 don't: that's the particle-level root of the greenhouse effect.

What we observe

Earth's surface, at an average of 287 K, emits infrared radiation. CO2 in the atmosphere absorbs some of it and re-emits it in all directions, so part of the heat is sent back down (PDF p.217–218).

What we write

CO2's three vibrations (Fig. 4.13): symmetric stretch, infrared-inactive; asymmetric stretch, infrared-active; bend, infrared-active.

What the particles are doing

Each C=O bond is polar, δ− on O and δ+ on C. Stretching or bending changes the separation of charge, unless two equal changes in opposite directions cancel.

Source: textbook §4.9, PDF p.217–219 (printed 183–185). Not yet taught in lecture: §4.9 is plain, not bold, in the Day 9 section list (Day 9 p.4). The professor lists the topic as Ch. 5 outcome 5, “Explain how molecules of some atmospheric gases absorb infrared radiation and contribute to the Greenhouse effect,” also not bold (Day 10 p.5; Day 11 p.5), while this edition of the textbook covers it in §4.9.

Understand

IR-active ⇔ the vibration changes the molecule's charge separation

Mechanism “As polar bonds vibrate, the strengths of tiny electrical fields produced by the partial separations of charge in the bonds fluctuate at the same frequencies as their vibrations. The natural frequencies of the vibrations correspond to frequencies of infrared radiation” (PDF p.217). A photon at that frequency can interact with the fluctuating field and be absorbed.

CO2 In the symmetric stretch “the two C=O bonds stretch and then compress at the same time. The two fluctuating electrical fields produced by the two C=O bonds cancel each other out,” so it's infrared-inactive. In the asymmetric stretch, one bond shortens as the other lengthens, and the changes don't cancel; the bend also makes the field fluctuate (PDF p.217–218).

Nonpolar bonds N2 and O2, about 99% of the atmosphere, have bonds between identical atoms. Their stretches create no fluctuating field (the textbook's Concept Test, PDF p.218).

Why that traps heat: after absorbing a photon, a molecule re-emits one of the same energy, and emitted photons “are just as likely to move back toward Earth's surface as they are to go upward toward space” (PDF p.217). Because CO2's active vibrations match the frequencies Earth emits, “carbon dioxide is a potent greenhouse gas” (PDF p.218).

Wien's law puts the peak of a 287 K surface's emission at λmax = (2.898 × 10−3 m·K)/287 K ≈ 10 μm, well into the infrared.

Connected lecture ideas

Infrared light has longer wavelengths and lower photon energies than visible light (E = hν = hc/λ, §3.1). Which bonds are polar comes from electronegativity, now taught (Day 9 p.15–18, §4.2). Day 10 adds the molecule-level picture: “Each BOND in CO2 is polar (Δχ = 1.0). But the two dipole moments are perfectly opposed. The molecule is nonpolar!” (Day 10 p.29, §5.3).

That cancellation is exactly what CO2's symmetric stretch preserves and its asymmetric stretch and bend break: a vibration that makes the two bond dipoles stop canceling, even for an instant, can absorb infrared light.

Recognize it

  • “Greenhouse gas,” “absorbs infrared” → look for a vibration that changes the charge separation.
  • Two identical atoms (N2, O2, Cl2) → no IR absorption from stretching.
  • A symmetric molecule's symmetric stretch → the changes cancel; an unsymmetric one → they don't.

Covered in lecture: Day 10 p.3, p.6–13Includes a boxed textbook preview

Builds on: §4.4 Lewis structures: the five steps, §4.8 Exceptions to the octet rule

Learn

A Lewis structure shows which atoms are bonded, not how they sit in space. VSEPR theory gets the 3-D shape from the Lewis structure: the electron pairs around a central atom repel one another, so they spread out as far as they can. Count the regions of electron density around the central atom, its steric number, and the shape follows.

What we observe

Day 10 p.6 pairs spearmint and caraway with two forms of carvone, “chiral molecules” with the same atoms bonded the same way: “Molecular geometries are clearly more complicated than Lewis structures” (Day 10 p.6; more in §5.6). The two forms are why spearmint and caraway smell and taste different (textbook Fig. 5.1, PDF p.232). Measured bond angles are 180° in CO2 and 109.5° in CH4 (Day 10 p.7).

What we write

“Sometimes Lewis structures are enough…” (Day 10 p.7): the straight O=C=O matches CO2's 180° shape, but CH4's flat cross shows 90° angles the molecule doesn't have.

What the electrons are doing

Every bonding pair and lone pair on the central atom is a cloud of negative charge. The clouds repel and settle as far apart as possible. For CH4's four bonds that means pointing to the corners of a tetrahedron, 109.5° apart, not to the corners of a flat square (Day 10 p.8–11).

  • No lone pairs on the central atom → the electron-pair and molecular geometries have the same name.
  • Five neighbors → two kinds of position (axial and equatorial); six → all alike.
  • A double bond next to single bonds → the angles between the single bonds close up a little.

Lone pairs: electron-pair vs. molecular geometry

Covered in lecture: Day 10 p.14–26Includes a boxed textbook preview

Lecture: Day 10 p.14–26. Textbook: §5.2 (lone pairs), printed pp. 203–209 (PDF 237–243).

Builds on: §5.1–5.2 Molecular shape and VSEPR: steric number, §4.5 Resonance

Learn

Lone pairs on the central atom count toward the steric number, so they help set the electron-pair geometry. But the molecular geometry describes the positions of the “atoms only” (Day 10 p.14), so a molecule with lone pairs gets a different shape name. Lone pairs also crowd the bonding pairs and shrink the bond angles.

What we observe

Measured bond angles: ozone 117° (Day 10 p.15), ammonia 107° (p.18), water 104.5° (p.19). Each is smaller than the ideal angle of its electron-pair geometry: 120°, 109.5°, and 109.5°.

What we write

Ozone's central O: 2 atoms + 1 lone pair. Ammonia's N: 3 + 1. Water's O: 2 + 2. The drawings are flat; the shapes come from the counts.

What the electrons are doing

The slide draws ozone's lone pair as a large lobe pressing on the two smaller bonding-pair lobes and squeezing them together (Day 10 p.15). The textbook's reason: bonding pairs are pulled toward two nuclei, while a lone pair stays close to the central atom and takes up more room there (PDF p.238).

Source: Day 10 p.14–19.

Polar bonds and polar molecules

Covered in lecture: Day 10 p.27–31; Day 11 p.6–8Includes a boxed textbook preview

Lecture: Day 10 p.27–31; Day 11 p.6–8. Textbook: §5.3, printed pp. 209–212 (PDF 243–246).

Builds on: §4.2 Electronegativity and polar bonds, §5.2 Lone pairs: electron-pair vs. molecular geometry

Learn

A polar bond has a δ+ end and a δ− end. A molecule is polar only if its bond dipoles don't cancel, and whether they cancel depends on its shape. So polar bonds aren't enough: “Each BOND in CO2 is polar … The molecule is nonpolar!” (Day 10 p.29).

What we observe

Electrostatic potential maps (red = negative, blue = positive; the color key is on Day 9 p.16): CO2 is colored the same at both O ends, while water is red at its O end and green-yellow at its H end (Day 10 p.29–31). Water's measured dipole moment is 1.85 D (Table 5.2, Day 11 p.8).

What we write

A crossed arrow, + at the tail, points along each bond to its more electronegative atom (Day 10 p.27). CO2: two arrows point outward, perfectly opposed (Day 10 p.29). H2O is drawn here in a straight line, as Lewis structures often are, but the molecule is bent (104.5°, Day 10 p.19): both arrows point partly toward O and add to one net arrow (Day 10 p.31).

What the electrons are doing

O pulls the shared pairs toward itself in every one of these bonds. In linear CO2 the two pulls are equal and opposite, so the charge stays balanced. In bent H2O both pulls lean the same way, toward O, and the molecule has a negative end and a positive end.

Source: Day 9 p.16; Day 10 p.19, p.27–31; Day 11 p.6, p.8.

molecular dipole = the bond dipoles added, direction included

cancel

Polar bonds (Day 10 p.27, the Day 9 p.15 picture again): the crossed arrow over H–Cl points to Cl, like a battery whose + end is H (δ+) and whose − end is Cl (δ−). Bond polarity and Δχ are in §4.2.

Definitions (PDF p.243–244): a bond dipole is the “separation of electrical charge created when atoms with different electronegativities form a covalent bond”; a permanent dipole is a “permanent separation of electrical charge in a molecule resulting from unequal distributions of bonding and/or lone pairs of electrons”; the dipole moment (μ) is “a measure of the degree to which a molecule aligns itself in an applied electric field; a quantitative expression of the polarity of a molecule.”

Adding dipoles: the summing “must take into account both the strengths of the individual bond dipoles and their orientations with respect to one another” (PDF p.243). The word permanent matters because vibrating bonds create temporary dipoles even in CO2, which is how it absorbs infrared radiation (PDF p.244; §4.9).

  • Two kinds of outer atom (CHCl3 and CCl3F, Day 11 p.7–8) → polar in the lecture's examples, and “highly likely” polar in general (the textbook's rule of thumb, PDF p.246).

Valence bond theory and hybrid orbitals

Covered in lecture: Day 11 p.9–16, p.20, p.24Includes a boxed textbook preview

Lecture: Day 11 p.9–16, p.20, p.24. Textbook: §5.4 (valence bond theory; sp³, sp², sp), printed pp. 212–219 (PDF 246–253).

Builds on: §3.7 Orbital shapes and radial distributions, §3.8 Electron configurations, §5.2 Lone pairs: electron-pair vs. molecular geometry

Learn

Lewis structures and VSEPR say where the electron pairs are. Valence bond theory says which orbitals hold them: a covalent bond forms when half-filled orbitals on two atoms overlap (Day 11 p.9). To point its bonds the way VSEPR predicts, an atom first mixes (“averages”) its valence s and p orbitals into hybrid orbitals, one for every electron domain: “the number of hybridized orbitals equals the steric number of the atom” (Day 11 p.12).

Methane's four C–H bonds are equivalent (Day 11 p.11), and every H–C–H angle is 109.5° (Day 10 p.7). Yet a carbon atom in its ground state has only two unpaired electrons, in 2p orbitals at right angles to each other (Day 11 p.11).

Understand

Valence bond theory (Day 11 p.9–10)

“VSEPR grew out of Lewis structures, which pre-date our understanding of atomic orbitals. Can we reconcile the two ideas? Valence bond theory (Linus Pauling, late 1920s) suggests that covalent bonds form when half-filled orbitals on different atoms overlap. The electrons in overlapping orbitals are attracted to the nuclei of both bonded atoms, increasing stability” (Day 11 p.9). The slide pairs this with the H–H energy curve from Day 8 p.11: as two H atoms approach, their half-filled 1s orbitals overlap, and the energy is lowest, −436 kJ/mol, with the nuclei 74 pm apart.

“Pauling got around this by suggesting the idea of hybridization: The mixing of atomic orbitals to generate new sets of orbitals that are then available to form covalent bonds with other atoms. Because orbitals are “just” mathematical functions, you can do anything to them that you can do to any other math function – such as averaging them. Covered in lecture: Day 11 p.17–26Includes a boxed textbook preview

Learn

“Each double bond consists of a sigma bond AND a pi (π) bond” (Day 11 p.17), and a triple bond is one σ bond and two π bonds. σ bonds come from head-on overlap of hybrid orbitals (or H's 1s); π bonds come from side-to-side overlap of the unhybridized p orbitals left over after hybridizing, with their electron density above and below the bond axis (Day 11 p.20). In a molecule with several “central” atoms, work out each atom's steric number and hybridization in turn.

What we observe

What we write

One line of each double bond is σ and the other is π. Day 11 p.19 colors formaldehyde's σ bond lines green and its π bond line blue.

What the electrons are doing

Each carbon in ethylene uses three sp2 hybrids for σ bonds (to two H atoms and the other C) and keeps one 2p orbital perpendicular to the plane of those three bonds. When the two carbons' 2p orbitals are parallel, they overlap side by side, above and below the C–C axis: one π bond with two lobes (Day 11 p.25).

Chirality and molecular recognition

Covered in lecture: Day 10 p.3, p.6Includes a boxed textbook preview

Lecture: Day 10 p.3, p.6. Textbook: §5.6, printed pp. 221–227 (PDF 255–261).

Builds on: §5.1–5.2 Molecular shape and VSEPR: steric number, §5.4 Valence bond theory and hybrid orbitals

Learn

Two molecules can have the same formula and the same Lewis structure and still be different substances, because their atoms are arranged differently in three dimensions. The lecture's example: “Molecular geometries are clearly more complicated than Lewis structures. One example is the existence of chiral molecules, like R(−) and S(+) carvone” (Day 10 p.6). One carvone smells of spearmint; its mirror image smells of caraway.

What we observe

Spearmint tea and caraway-seed bread, the photos on Day 10 p.6, owe their smells to the two carvones, both C10H14O. Our noses tell them apart (textbook §5.1, PDF p.232).

What we write

A Lewis structure is flat: this one drawing of CHBrClF fits both of its mirror-image forms. To show which form you mean, draw wedges (bonds toward you) and dashes (bonds away), as the slide's carvone drawings do (Day 10 p.6).

What the particles are doing

An sp3 carbon points its four bonds at the corners of a tetrahedron. When all four groups differ, there are two arrangements, mirror images of each other, and no rotation turns one into the other, just as no rotation turns a left hand into a right hand.

Chirality in nature (PDF p.260). Molecular orbital theory

Covered in lecture: Day 11 p.27; Day 12 p.4–13Includes a boxed textbook preview

Lecture: Day 11 p.27; Day 12 p.4–13. Textbook: §5.7, printed pp. 227–239 (PDF 261–273).

Learn

Liquid oxygen sticks to a magnet, and “Only molecules with unpaired electrons are paramagnetic” (Day 11 p.27, repeated on Day 12 p.6). Yet the Lewis structure O=O pairs every electron, and the models built on it can't do better: “Lewis structures, VSEPR, and valence bond/hybridization theories ALL fail to predict this.” Molecular orbital (MO) theory explains it. It takes “ALL the valence atomic orbitals from the individual atoms” and spreads them over the whole molecule (Day 12 p.7), and O2's last two electrons land, unpaired, in two orbitals of equal energy (Day 12 p.11).

What we observe

Liquid O2 poured between the poles of a magnet hangs there, held by the field (the photo on Day 11 p.27 and Day 12 p.6; textbook Fig. 5.51, PDF p.267). Oxygen is paramagnetic: it is attracted to a magnetic field.

What we write

The Lewis structure (Day 8 p.20), with every electron paired, and O2's MO diagram (Day 12 p.11), which the textbook writes as a configuration (PDF p.265): (σ2s)2(σ*2s)2(σ2p)2(π2p)4(π*2p)2, with two unpaired electrons.

What the electrons are doing

O2's 12 valence electrons fill molecular orbitals from the lowest energy up. The last two go into a pair of π*2p orbitals of equal energy, one in each, with parallel spins: Hund's rule, as for carbon's two 2p electrons (Day 6 p.16). Day 12 p.11 circles those two electrons in red.

Recognize it

  • “Paramagnetic,” “attracted to a magnet,” or “unpaired electrons” for a diatomic → fill its MO diagram (Day 12 p.11).
  • The bond order of a diatomic molecule or ion → count the valence electrons (one more per unit of negative charge, one fewer per unit of positive charge), fill from the bottom, then ½(bonding − antibonding) (Day 12 p.8–9).
  • Which order? Li2 through N2: π2p below σ2p. O2 through Ne2: σ2p below π2p (Day 12 p.11). The textbook uses the O2 order for NO and fills an ion with its parent molecule's order (preview below).
  • Bond order 0 → no net bond, so the molecule isn't expected to exist (He2, Be2, Ne2; Day 12 p.9, p.11).
  • Highest filled or lowest empty MO → HOMO or LUMO (Day 12 p.11).
  • A magnetic or spectroscopic question → MO theory; connectivity → Lewis structures; 3-D shape → VSEPR or valence bond theory (Day 12 p.13).

Definitions. The textbook defines MO theory as “a bonding theory based on the mixing of atomic orbitals of similar shapes and energies to form molecular orbitals that belong to the molecule as a whole” (PDF p.260). Bonding MOs have “lobes of high electron density that lie between bonded pairs of atoms” and stabilize the molecule; antibonding MOs lie higher in energy and destabilize it (PDF p.261).

The five guidelines for building a diagram (PDF p.263–264). The slides state the first (Day 12 p.7) and fill their diagrams by the last two (Day 12 p.9, p.11):

  1. The number of molecular orbitals equals the number of atomic orbitals used to create them.
  2. Atomic orbitals with similar energy and orientation mix more effectively than do those with different energies and orientations.
  3. Better mixing leads to a larger energy difference between bonding and antibonding orbitals and thus greater stabilization of the bonding MOs.
  4. A molecular orbital can accommodate a maximum of two electrons; two electrons in the same MO have opposite spins.
  5. Electrons in ground-state molecules occupy the lowest-energy molecular orbitals available, following the aufbau principle and Hund's rule.

Why the order switches (Fig. 5.49, PDF p.265–266). The energy gap between 2s and 2p grows with atomic number. For Li2 through N2 (Z ≤ 7) it is small enough for some mixing of 2s and 2p, which “raises the energy of the σ2p orbital—enough to put it above the π2p orbital” (PDF p.266).

Magnetism (PDF p.266). The textbook names the opposite case: a substance whose molecules have every electron paired is diamagnetic, weakly repelled by a magnetic field. “The more unpaired electrons in a molecule, the greater the paramagnetism. Only MO theory accounts for the magnetic behavior of oxygen and other molecular substances.”

Ions (Sample Ex. 5.8, PDF p.267). An ion is filled with its parent molecule's order: add or remove electrons at the top of the diagram. Removing an antibonding electron raises the bond order (O2 2 → O2+ 2.5); removing a bonding one lowers it (N2 3 → N2+ 2.5).

Two different atoms: NO (Fig. 5.52, PDF p.268–269). O's atomic orbitals lie lower in energy than N's. Bonding MOs end up closer to the more electronegative atom's orbitals (O) and antibonding MOs closer to the less electronegative atom's (N), with σ2p below π2p, the O2 order. NO's 11 valence electrons leave one in π*2p: bond order ½(8 − 3) = 2.5, “halfway between the bond orders for N=O and N≡O,” which fits its 115 pm bond, halfway between N=O (122 pm) and N≡O (106 pm). The odd electron sits mostly on N, as in the Lewis structure: NO+ has bond order 3 and NO− 2 (Sample Ex. 5.9).

NO is the textbook's only two-element diagram. For some other two-element molecules, such as CO and CN−, detailed calculations put π2p below σ2p, as in N2. The bond orders and magnetism this section's problems ask for come out the same with either order.

Colors of auroras (Table 5.4, PDF p.269–270). Electrons can jump between MOs, absorbing or emitting UV or visible light, just as electrons in atoms do (the line spectra on Day 3 p.8–10). Builds on: §5.7 Molecular orbital theory, §4.1 Bonds, Coulomb energy, and lattices, §3.8 Electron configurations, §4.4 Lewis symbols and the octet rule

Learn

A copper wire carries current, a diamond doesn't, and silicon carries a little, more when it's warm (Day 12 p.17, p.24). Day 12 explains all three with molecular orbitals. “We'll get one molecular orbital out for every atomic orbital that we put in” (Day 12 p.7), so a piece of metal, with an enormous number of atoms, has an enormous number of MOs, so close in energy that they merge into a band. How a solid's bands are filled decides whether it conducts: “Any material with a partially filled valence band or a filled valence band that overlaps with an empty conduction band is an electrical conductor. Any material with a large enough band gap acts as an insulator” (Day 12 p.18).

What we observe

Metals are “Malleable: can be formed into thin sheets,” “Ductile: can be pulled into thin wires,” lustrous, and “Heat and electricity conductors”; all but mercury are solids at room temperature, and they “Tend to form cations” (Day 12 p.17). Between the metals and the nonmetals on the periodic table sit the metalloids, B, Si, Ge, As, Sb, Te, and At (Day 12 p.16). Diamond is an insulator, zinc and sodium are conductors, and silicon is a semiconductor (Day 12 p.24).

What we write

A band diagram: energy up the page, each band drawn as a box, shaded where electrons fill it, as in the professor's sketch (Day 12 p.24). The band that holds the valence electrons is the valence band; an empty band above it is the conduction band; the energy between them, if there is any, is the band gap, Eg (the labels on Day 12 p.22–25).

What the electrons are doing

Each Na atom, [Ne]3s1, brings one 3s electron. N atoms make N MOs from their 3s orbitals, each spread over the whole piece of metal, and the N electrons fill the lower half, two per MO: a “Valence band (partially filled)” (Day 12 p.22). In zinc, [Ar]3d104s2, two 4s electrons per atom fill the 4s band, but the empty band made from the 4p orbitals overlaps it (Day 12 p.23).

Understand

From two atoms to a metal (Day 12 p.19–22). Two Na atoms each bring a half-filled 3s orbital. Together they make two MOs: a bonding MO below the 3s energy, which holds both electrons, and an empty MO above it (“MO Theory: Na2,” p.19). The energy between them is the HOMO–LUMO gap (p.20); HOMO is the “Highest Occupied Molecular Orbital” and LUMO the “Lowest Unoccupied Molecular Orbital” (p.11). Four atoms give four MOs, two filled and two empty, and “As we add more and more atoms (and electrons) to the MO diagram, the HOMO-LUMO gap gets smaller quickly. Pretty soon, the words “HOMO” and “LUMO” begin to become meaningless” (p.21). In NaN, a real piece of sodium, the levels merge into a continuous band, the lower half occupied and the upper half empty, and this 3s band overlaps the empty band made from the 3p orbitals (p.22).

Why metals bend instead of breaking. Malleability is “the ability to be shaped” and ductility “the ability to be drawn out”; “One reason that metals have both malleability and ductility … is that the bonds between atoms in a solid are weak” (PDF p.918). Solid sodium is body-centered cubic: each Na atom is surrounded by eight others and shares its single 3s electron with all eight, and “sharing a limited number of valence electrons with many bonding partners makes the bond linking any two metal atoms relatively weak.” Sodium is soft enough to cut with a knife (Fig. 18.24). The Ch. 4 sea of electrons “certainly fits,” but band theory, “an extension of molecular orbital theory,” explains more (PDF p.918).

Why a band needs empty room. In sodium's half-filled band, “valence electrons can move easily from the filled lower portion to the empty upper portion, where they can move from one empty orbital to the next and flow throughout the solid” (PDF p.919). Zinc's filled 4s band has “no empty space … to accommodate additional electrons,” so on its own its electrons would be immobile; but the empty 4p conduction band “is broad enough to overlap the valence band,” and electrons move into it (Fig. 18.26). The two pictures “are not mutually exclusive”: zinc's overlapping 4s and 4p bands can also be read as one partially filled band (PDF p.919).

Definitions (margins, PDF p.920–921): band theory, “an extension of molecular orbital theory that describes bonding in solids”; valence band, “a band of orbitals that are filled or partially filled by valence electrons”; conduction band, “an unoccupied band higher in energy than a valence band in which electrons can migrate”; band gap (Eg), “the energy gap between the valence and conduction bands”; semiconductor, “a material with electrical conductivity between that of metals and insulators that can be chemically altered to increase its electrical conductivity”; insulator, “a material with a large energy gap between its valence and conduction bands.”

Silicon in numbers (PDF p.920). Metalloids conduct worse than metals but better than nonmetals because their bands “do not overlap but instead are separated by an energy gap.” For silicon, Eg = 106 kJ/mol at 25 °C, and “only a few valence-band electrons in Si have enough energy to move to the conduction band.”

Doping (PDF p.920). Replacing some Si atoms with atoms “having a similar atomic radius but a different number of valence electrons” is doping, and the added element is the dopant; doped semiconductors “represent substitutional alloys.” Phosphorus's extra electrons occupy a narrow donor level “only about 4 kJ/mol below the Si conduction band,” so they reach the conduction band easily: an n-type semiconductor, “so called because the dopant donates negative charges (electrons).” Gallium's acceptor level lies “about 7 kJ/mol above the Si valence band.” Valence electrons that move up into it leave behind “positively charged ‘holes’” (the ⊕ on Day 12 p.25), and electrons hopping from hole to hole carry current: a p-type semiconductor. Electronic devices combine the two types.

Insulators and compound semiconductors (PDF p.920–921). NaCl is an insulator with “an enormous band gap,” while zinc has “essentially no band gap.” Compounds of group 13 and group 15 elements, such as gallium arsenide (GaAs, an average of (3 + 5)/2 = 4 valence electrons per atom, like Si), are semiconductors with larger gaps. GaAs emits infrared light (874 nm) when electrons fall from the conduction band back to the valence band, and related materials make light-emitting diodes (LEDs); gallium nitride gives blue.

Textbook slip in §18.5. The book gives NaCl's band gap as “6.8 × 105 kJ/mol” (PDF p.920). That would be about 7 × 103 eV per electron, an X-ray energy; NaCl's measured gap is about 8.5–9 eV, roughly 8 × 102 kJ/mol (a background value), so the printed number is probably 6.8 × 105 J/mol. Either way it is several times silicon's 106 kJ/mol. The caption of Fig. 18.27 also puts the Ga level “just above the Si valence electrons,” meaning the valence band.

Recognize it

  • “Conducts electricity,” “insulator,” “semiconductor,” “band gap” → band theory, not one molecule's MO diagram (Day 12 p.13, p.18).
  • One valence s electron per atom, as in Na → a partly filled band → a conductor. A filled valence s subshell, as in Zn → a conductor only because an empty band overlaps it.
  • A metalloid such as Si → a small gap → a semiconductor, which conducts better when heated or doped.
  • A dopant from the group to the right of the host (more valence electrons) → n-type, donor level just below the conduction band. From the group to the left (fewer) → p-type, acceptor level just above the valence band.

Common mistakes

  • Thinking a filled band conducts on its own: zinc conducts only because the empty 4p band overlaps its filled 4s band (Day 12 p.23).
  • Counting MOs as electrons ÷ 2: N atomic orbitals give N MOs, filled or not (p.7, p.21).
  • Mixing up n and p: n-type gets extra electrons from a dopant with more valence electrons than the host (P in Si); p-type gets vacancies from one with fewer (Ga in Si) (p.25).
  • Believing that heating closes a semiconductor's gap: in the “T ↑” sketch the gap is unchanged, and more electrons cross it (p.24).
  • Applying HOMO and LUMO to a whole metal: once the levels form a band, the words “begin to become meaningless” (p.21).
