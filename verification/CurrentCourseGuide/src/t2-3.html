Learn

The periodic table is ordered by atomic number and arranged so that elements with similar chemistry fall in the same column. Rows are periods; columns are groups. The position tells you whether an element is a metal, nonmetal, or metalloid, and often which ion it forms.

What we observe

Metals are shiny, malleable, ductile conductors; all but mercury are solids. Nonmetals conduct poorly, and most are gases; bromine is a liquid. Metalloids are in between.

What we write

Groups 1, 2, 13–18 are main-group (representative) elements; 3–12 are transition metals. Common ion charges: groups 1 → 1+, 2 → 2+, 13 → 3+, 15 → 3−, 16 → 2−, 17 → 1− (Table 2.2).

Why it works

Group members share a valence-electron pattern, and valence electrons “primarily determine the chemical properties” (Day 6 p.14, covered in lecture). That's why Li, Na, and K all form 1+ ions.

Understand

Textbook preview Mendeleev (1872) ordered elements by atomic mass, grouped similar ones, and left gaps, predicting the properties of undiscovered elements. The modern table orders by Z: 7 periods and 18 groups, with the lanthanides (Z = 58–71) and actinides (90–103) set below. Every isotope beyond Z = 83 is radioactive.

Common mistakes

  • Mixing up periods (rows) and groups (columns).
  • Calling hydrogen an alkali metal.
  • Assigning ion charges to groups 14 and 18 by the pattern.
