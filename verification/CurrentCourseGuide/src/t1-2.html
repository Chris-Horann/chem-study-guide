Learn

COAST is the textbook's four-step habit for problems: Collect and Organize, Analyze, Solve, Think about it. It exists to stop the most common failure: grabbing an equation whose symbols look right and plugging numbers in.

What it looks like

Before any arithmetic, a good solver writes down what's given, what's asked, and a rough estimate of the answer.

What you write

Givens with units → a plan connecting them, often worked backward from the answer's units → the calculation → a check against the estimate.

Where the chemistry is

Analyze is where you decide what is physically happening (a photon? an ion pair? a mole of atoms?). That decision picks the relationship.

Understand

StepWhat you doQuestions to ask
Collect and OrganizeIdentify the concept; sort relevant from irrelevant information; gather equations, definitions, constants.What is this problem about? What is given, with units? What is asked?
AnalyzeConnect the givens to the answer, sometimes working backward; note the unit conversions you'll need; sketch; estimate an order of magnitude.Which relationship links these quantities? Do the units match what it needs?
SolveSubstitute and calculate with consistent units; round at the end to justified significant figures.Do the units cancel to the unit I want?
Think About ItCompare with the estimate; check units and significant figures; ask whether you could solve a related problem.Is this reasonable? If not, where is the error?

The textbook calls COAST “merely a framework for solving problems, not a recipe.” For simple problems it merges the first two steps. When Think About It fails, go back to Analyze.

The lecture never uses COAST; the slides grey out §1.2 (Day 1 p.6). But the lecture's worked examples show the same habits: units written into every factor and g converted to kg before calculating (Day 4 p.12).
