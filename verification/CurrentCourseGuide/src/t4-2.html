Learn

“Even when G.N. Lewis was developing the rules for his Lewis structures, he was aware that the electrons in covalent bonds didn't have to be shared equally. In many (most?) covalent bonds, one of the atoms has more of the electron density” (Day 9 p.15). Electronegativity, χ, describes that pull. The difference Δχ places every bond on one scale: nonpolar covalent, polar covalent, ionic (Day 9 p.17–18).

What we observe

A polar bond has a positive end and a negative end, like a battery. The slide lines H–Cl up with an AA battery: the H end at the + terminal (δ+), the Cl end at the − terminal (δ−) (Day 9 p.15). The same picture opens Day 10's lesson on polar molecules (Day 10 p.27).

What we write

Hδ+–Clδ−, or a crossed arrow over the bond with its + tail at H and its head at Cl, the δ− end (Day 9 p.15).

What the electrons are doing

The potential maps color the charge (Day 9 p.16). Cl2: “Nonpolar covalent: even charge distribution.” HCl: “Polar covalent: uneven charge distribution,” with the shared pair pulled toward Cl. NaCl: “Ionic: complete transfer of electron,” Na+ and Cl−.

Source: Day 9 p.15–18; Day 10 p.27.

Understand

Δχ = |χA − χB|

χ (electronegativity)
“To describe the polarity of bonds, we will introduce the concept of electronegativity, given the symbol χ” (Day 9 p.17). The slide gives values, not a definition: a table of bars, tallest at the upper right (F 4.0).
Δχ
the difference between the two bonded atoms' values, taken as positive

The slide's cutoffs (Day 9 p.18): Δχ ≤ 0.4 nonpolar covalent · 0.4 < Δχ < 2.0 polar covalent · Δχ ≥ 2.0 ionic.

Partial charges δ− goes on the atom with the larger χ, where the arrowhead points; the other atom is δ+.

Values from the course's table (Day 9 p.17): H 2.1 · Li 1.1, Be 1.5, B 2.0, C 2.5, N 3.0, O 3.5, F 4.0 · Na 0.9, Mg 1.2, Al 1.5, Si 1.8, P 2.1, S 2.5, Cl 3.0 · K 0.8, Ca 1.0, Br 2.8, I 2.5. No values are given for the noble gases.

The maps' color bar runs from dark blue “1+” (“100% ionic”) through light blue δ+, green-yellow 0 (“Nonpolar covalent”), and orange δ−, to dark red “1−” (“100% ionic”) (Day 9 p.16). Blue means positive, red negative.

One color bar covers all three maps, so ionic bonding is the far end of unequal sharing rather than a separate kind of thing; the slides show this without saying it. The same colors return in Day 10's maps of CO2, CF4, and H2O (Day 10 p.29–31).

Connected lecture ideas

Textbook preview (§4.2, PDF p.185–188, printed 151–154)

A definition. The slides give χ no verbal definition. The textbook: electronegativity is “a relative measure of an atom's ability to attract electrons to itself within a bond” (PDF p.187).

Guidelines, not walls. The textbook uses the same cutoffs and adds that “those cutoff values are more like guidelines than strict limits.” Its examples: calcium oxide, Δχ = 3.5 − 1.0 = 2.5, ionic; Cl–Br, Δχ = 0.2, essentially nonpolar (PDF p.186).

The trend and its reason. χ increases across a row and decreases down a group, like first ionization energy (Fig. 4.6, PDF p.187): “Greater attraction between the nuclei of atoms and their outer-shell electrons produces both higher ionization energies and greater electronegativities across a row” (PDF p.187). In Ch. 3's terms (our gloss, not the textbook's wording here), that means a higher Zeff and a smaller atom; Day 7 p.9 shows the matching IE₁ trend. The most electronegative elements (F, O, N, Cl) sit at the upper right; the least (Fr, Cs, Rb, K) at the lower left. The slides show the table but don't make the comparison.

Li is 1.1 on the course's table (Day 9 p.17), as in the textbook's Fig. 4.5; many other tables list 1.0, so the problems avoid depending on it.

Recognize it

  • “Is this bond polar?” or “which end is δ−?” → look up both χ values and take Δχ.
  • Δχ right at a cutoff: exactly 0.4 is still nonpolar (≤), and exactly 2.0 is already ionic (≥).
  • Different elements with equal χ, such as C and S (both 2.5) → a nonpolar bond, even though the atoms differ.
  • Metal + nonmetal is a hint, not a verdict: compute Δχ.

Common mistakes

  • Treating a Δχ just under 2.0 as ionic.
  • Putting δ− on the less electronegative atom, or pointing the arrowhead at the δ+ end.
  • Using one element's χ in place of Δχ.
  • Deciding ionic or covalent from metal and nonmetal alone.
