Learn

Dalton's indivisible atom broke open in three experiments. Thomson found tiny negative particles inside every atom (electrons, 1897). Millikan measured the electron's charge (1909). Rutherford's group fired α particles at gold foil and found that nearly all of an atom's mass and all of its positive charge sit in a tiny nucleus (1911).

What we observe

A cathode-ray beam bends toward a + plate and in a magnetic field (Day 2 p.7–8). Most α particles pass straight through thin gold foil; a few bounce back (Day 2 p.15, p.18): “as if you had fired a 15-inch shell at a piece of tissue paper and it came back and hit you.”

What we write

qe = −1.602 × 10−19 C; me = 9.109 × 10−28 g (Day 2 p.12). Proton 1.00728 amu, neutron 1.00866 amu, electron 5.48580 × 10−4 amu (Day 2 p.22). Nucleus ~0.01 pm across in an atom ~288 pm across (Day 2 p.20).

What the particles are doing

Plum pudding: diffuse positive charge with embedded electrons, which can't turn an α particle around (Day 2 p.11, p.16). Nuclear atom: a tiny, dense, positive nucleus in a diffuse electron cloud. The rare α particle aimed nearly head-on at a nucleus is repelled back (Day 2 p.17–19).

Source: Day 1 p.18–20; Day 2 p.4–24.

The fuller treatment is in §2.2–2.4 below.

Textbook preview (§2.1 Radioactivity, PDF p.84–85, printed 50–51)

Becquerel (1896) found uranium ore emitting radiation; the Curies and Rutherford showed it was several kinds. Radioactivity is the spontaneous emission of high-energy radiation and particles. β particles are high-energy electrons (1−). α particles are 4He nuclei (2+) and are much more massive; the two bend in opposite directions in a magnetic field. The textbook adds that about 1 in 8000 α particles in the gold-foil experiment was deflected by an average of 90°.

Understand

Thomson's reasoning (Day 2 p.10)

  • Deflected by a magnet → the beam's particles have mass and charge.
  • Deflected toward the + plate → negatively charged.
  • The electric and magnetic effects “pitted against each other” → the particles are TINY. Balancing the two deflections gives the charge-to-mass ratio; the slide doesn't use that term.

particle-mass comparison: mp / me = 1.00728 ÷ (5.48580 × 10−4) ≈ 1836

The lecture says a hydrogen atom is “2000 times” heavier than an electron (Day 2 p.12); the precise ratio is about 1837. Electrons are far too light to turn an α particle back.

Day 2 p.19 says the nucleus is “about 1/10,000” the size of the atom, while the figure's numbers (0.01 pm vs. 288 pm) give about 1/29,000. Both are order-of-magnitude statements (~10−4).

Common mistakes

  • Thinking the bouncing α particles hit electrons.
  • Confusing charge with mass in the particle table.
  • Forgetting that Millikan measured the charge; the mass then followed from Thomson's ratio.
