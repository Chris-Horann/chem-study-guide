Learn

An element is defined by its number of protons, the atomic number Z. Atoms of one element can still differ in their number of neutrons: these are isotopes. A nuclide symbol packs it all in: AZX, where A (the mass number) counts protons plus neutrons. For an ion, a charge Q goes on the right: AXQ.

What we observe

Thomson and Aston sent Ne+ ions through electric and magnetic fields and saw two spots on a photographic plate: about 90% at 20 u and 10% at 22 u.

What we write

20Ne and 22Ne (neon-20, Ne-20); 16O2− has 8 p, 8 n, and 8 + 2 = 10 e−.

What the particles are doing

Same number of protons, so the same chemistry. Different numbers of neutrons, so different masses. An ion's charge comes from missing or extra electrons; the nucleus is unchanged.

Understand

AZXQ

Z
protons (atomic number); fixes the element. The subscript is often omitted because the symbol already implies it.
A
protons + neutrons (mass number, a count, not a mass)
neutrons
A − Z
Q
charge = protons − electrons, so electrons = Z − Q

For particles In ⁰₋₁e the subscript is the relative charge, which matches Z for a nucleus, since Z is the nucleus's charge.

Limits A ≥ Z always; only 1H has A = Z.

Textbook preview The textbook's history: Dalton's claim that all atoms of an element are identical had to be revised. An element is now defined by Z, and its atoms may differ in A.

Recognize it

  • A symbol with numbers and a count asked → protons = Z, neutrons = A − Z, electrons = Z − Q.
  • “Same element, different mass” → isotopes.

Common mistakes

  • Adding the charge to get electrons for a cation (a 3+ ion has 3 fewer electrons).
  • Treating A as the number of neutrons.
  • Identifying the element from the electron count instead of the proton count.
