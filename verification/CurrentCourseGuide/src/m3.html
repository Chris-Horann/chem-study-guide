Learn

Light is one member of a huge family, electromagnetic radiation, running from γ rays to radio waves. Every member travels at the same speed, c, so wavelength and frequency trade off exactly: λν = c. Each packet of light, a photon, carries energy E = hν = hc/λ. Shorter wavelength means more energy per photon.

What we observe

Colors from violet (about 400 nm) to red (about 750 nm); UV causes sunburn while microwaves only warm food; the spectrum runs from “Shortest wavelength (Highest energy)” to “Longest wavelength (Lowest energy)” (Day 2 p.29).

What we write

c = 2.998 × 108 m/s; λν = c; ν = c/λ; E = hν (Day 2 p.30), with h = 6.626 × 10−34 J·s (Day 3 p.17); E = hc/λ (Day 4 p.10).

What the light is doing

As a wave: crests pass a point ν times per second, λ apart (Day 2 p.30). As particles: energy arrives in photons of hν each (Day 3 p.17). Both descriptions describe the same light.

Source: Day 2 p.25–30; Day 3 p.12, p.17; Day 4 p.10.

Textbook preview (§3.1, PDF p.120–122, printed 86–88)

In Maxwell's theory, an electromagnetic wave is an electric field and a magnetic field oscillating at right angles to each other (textbook Fig. 3.2). Frequency is measured in hertz: 1 Hz = 1 s−1. The textbook opens the chapter with the aurora, where oxygen atoms excited by particles from the sun emit red and green light.

Why it holds (crests per second) × (meters per crest) = meters per second. With c fixed, doubling λ halves ν.

Use it when converting between the wavelength and frequency of any EM radiation.

Don't confuse with λ = h/(mu), which is for particles with mass (§3.5).

Recognize it

  • A wavelength or frequency of light, a color, or a spectral region → λν = c.
  • “Energy of a photon” or “per photon” → E = hν = hc/λ.
  • An answer in the radio range for “green light” → a units slip (nm left in).

Common mistakes

  • Leaving λ in nm when c is in m/s.
  • Thinking a longer wavelength means more energy.
  • Using E = hν for a moving electron (that's de Broglie territory).
