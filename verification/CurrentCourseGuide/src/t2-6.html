Learn

A mass spectrometer weighs molecules by turning them into ions and sorting the ions by mass-to-charge ratio (m/z). Most ions carry a 1+ charge, so each peak's position is the ion's mass. The molecular-ion peak, M+, gives the molecular mass; the fragment peaks and isotope patterns help identify the compound.

What we observe

Airport scanners swab luggage and identify explosives like TATP (M+ at m/z 222) from their mass spectra.

What we write

C6H6: 6(12.011) + 6(1.0079) = 78.11 u → M+ at m/z 78, with fragments at 51, 39, and others (textbook Fig. 2.22).

What the particles are doing

A high-energy electron knocks one electron out of a molecule, making M+. Harder hits break the molecule into fragment ions. Ions of different m/z bend by different amounts in the fields, exactly as Aston's neon ions did (§2.2).

Source: textbook §2.6, PDF p.104–108 (printed 70–74).

Understand

  • Axes: m/z (u) across the bottom; relative intensity, the number of ions, up the side.
  • Molecular ion: often the prominent peak with the highest mass, though not always the tallest peak.
  • Isotope peaks: HCl shows peaks at 36 (H35Cl) and 38 (H37Cl) because chlorine is about 76% 35Cl and 24% 37Cl.
  • Charge: a 2+ ion would appear at half its mass, which is why z matters.

The textbook prints carbon's mass as “12.001 u” in its acetylene and benzene sums, but the totals it reports (26.038 u, 78.113 u) use the correct 12.011 u.

This is the Thomson → Aston line of Ch. 2: charged particles deflected by fields (Day 2 p.6–10, covered in lecture) become a precise weighing instrument.
