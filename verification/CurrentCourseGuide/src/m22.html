Learn

A polar bond has a δ+ end and a δ− end. A molecule is polar only if its bond dipoles don't cancel, and whether they cancel depends on its shape. So polar bonds aren't enough: “Each BOND in CO2 is polar … The molecule is nonpolar!” (Day 10 p.29).

What we observe

Electrostatic potential maps (red = negative, blue = positive; the color key is on Day 9 p.16): CO2 is colored the same at both O ends, while water is red at its O end and green-yellow at its H end (Day 10 p.29–31). Water's measured dipole moment is 1.85 D (Table 5.2, Day 11 p.8).

What we write

A crossed arrow, + at the tail, points along each bond to its more electronegative atom (Day 10 p.27). CO2: two arrows point outward, perfectly opposed (Day 10 p.29). H2O is drawn here in a straight line, as Lewis structures often are, but the molecule is bent (104.5°, Day 10 p.19): both arrows point partly toward O and add to one net arrow (Day 10 p.31).

What the electrons are doing

O pulls the shared pairs toward itself in every one of these bonds. In linear CO2 the two pulls are equal and opposite, so the charge stays balanced. In bent H2O both pulls lean the same way, toward O, and the molecule has a negative end and a positive end.

Source: Day 9 p.16; Day 10 p.19, p.27–31; Day 11 p.6, p.8.

Understand

molecular dipole = the bond dipoles added, direction included

bond dipole
points to the more electronegative atom (δ−); the larger Δχ, the more polar the bond (course χ values, Day 9 p.17). Bonds with Δχ ≤ 0.4, such as C–H, count as nonpolar (Day 9 p.18), so Day 11 p.7 draws no arrow on C–H.
cancel
equal dipoles pointing in opposite directions, or spread evenly around the central atom, add up to zero

Method Lewis structure → VSEPR shape → which bonds are polar → do their dipoles cancel?

Limit Δχ tells you which way each bond dipole points and whether identical bonds cancel. It doesn't give the size of a molecule's dipole moment (CHCl3 and CCl3F, below).

Polar bonds (Day 10 p.27, the Day 9 p.15 picture again): the crossed arrow over H–Cl points to Cl, like a battery whose + end is H (δ+) and whose − end is Cl (δ−). Bond polarity and Δχ are in §4.2.

The lecture's three molecules

Carbon dioxide: “Each BOND in CO2 is polar (Δχ = 1.0). But the two dipole moments are perfectly opposed. The molecule is nonpolar!” (Day 10 p.29)

Carbon tetrafluoride: “Each BOND in CF4 is polar (Δχ = 1.5!) But all four dipole moments are perfectly opposed. The molecule is nonpolar!” (Day 10 p.30)

Water: “Each BOND in H2O is polar (Δχ = 1.4). But the dipole moments are not perfectly opposed. The molecule is polar!” (Day 10 p.31; shown again on Day 11 p.6)

The Δχ values come from the course table, C 2.5, O 3.5, F 4.0, H 2.1 (Day 9 p.17): C–O 1.0, C–F 1.5, O–H 1.4. CF4's bonds are the most polar of the three, and it's still nonpolar: shape decides. In a tetrahedron no two bonds point in exactly opposite directions, so “perfectly opposed” on the CF4 slide means the four dipoles cancel as a set: each one is balanced by the other three together.

Two kinds of outer atom: CHCl3 and CCl3F

Day 11 p.7 draws both molecules as tetrahedra with their bond dipoles: three C–Cl arrows and no arrow on C–H in CHCl3, and three C–Cl arrows plus a C–F arrow in CCl3F. C–H gets no arrow; its Δχ, 2.5 − 2.1 = 0.4, falls in the lecture's nonpolar range (“Δχ ≤ 0.4 nonpolar covalent,” Day 9 p.18). In CCl3F the C–F dipole points away from the three Cl atoms, against their combined dipole, so the two partly cancel. That's why CCl3F is only weakly polar.

Textbook preview (§5.3, PDF p.243–246, printed 209–212)

Definitions (PDF p.243–244): a bond dipole is the “separation of electrical charge created when atoms with different electronegativities form a covalent bond”; a permanent dipole is a “permanent separation of electrical charge in a molecule resulting from unequal distributions of bonding and/or lone pairs of electrons”; the dipole moment (μ) is “a measure of the degree to which a molecule aligns itself in an applied electric field; a quantitative expression of the polarity of a molecule.”

Adding dipoles: the summing “must take into account both the strengths of the individual bond dipoles and their orientations with respect to one another” (PDF p.243). The word permanent matters because vibrating bonds create temporary dipoles even in CO2, which is how it absorbs infrared radiation (PDF p.244; §4.9).

Measuring μ: gaseous HF molecules point every which way until an electric field is switched on; then they line up between the plates (Fig. 5.19). “The more polar a molecule, the more strongly it aligns with an electric field” (PDF p.245). Units: debyes, “1 D = 3.34 × 10−30 coulomb-meter” (PDF p.245). Chapter 5 gives no equation for μ.

C–H: “Because hydrogen is only slightly less electronegative than carbon (Δχ = 0.4), the H–C bond is considered essentially nonpolar” (PDF p.245).

Sample Ex. 5.4 (PDF p.245–246): formaldehyde's polar C=O bond gives it a permanent dipole; CH2Cl2 is polar however its Lewis structure is drawn, because “a Lewis structure simply depicts what bonds are present, not how those bonds are oriented in space.” Rule of thumb: “When more than one kind of atom is bonded to a central atom, the molecule is highly likely to have a permanent dipole.” A Concept Test asks why H2S (0.97 D) has a smaller dipole moment than H2O (1.85 D), though both are bent (PDF p.246).

Recognize it

  • “Is it polar?” → Lewis structure → shape → polar bonds → do the dipoles cancel?
  • Identical outer atoms and no lone pairs on the central atom (the five shapes of §5.1–5.2) → nonpolar, however polar the bonds.
  • Lone pairs that bend or tip the molecule (bent, trigonal pyramidal, seesaw, T-shaped, square pyramidal) → polar. Linear and square planar shapes are the exceptions: their lone pairs sit symmetrically, so identical bond dipoles still cancel.
  • Two kinds of outer atom (CHCl3 and CCl3F, Day 11 p.7–8) → polar in the lecture's examples, and “highly likely” polar in general (the textbook's rule of thumb, PDF p.246).

Common mistakes

  • Calling a molecule polar because its bonds are polar (CO2, CF4).
  • Judging polarity from the flat Lewis structure: H–O–H drawn in a line looks nonpolar.
  • Forgetting that a lone pair changes the shape (BF3 vs. NF3).
  • Expecting Δχ arrows to predict the size of μ.
