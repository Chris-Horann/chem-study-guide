Learn

Atoms bond in three ways. Ionic bonds hold oppositely charged ions together by electrostatic attraction. Covalent bonds share electrons between nonmetals. Metallic bonds share mobile electrons among metal atoms. For ions, Coulomb's law makes it quantitative: bigger charges and shorter distances give a more negative (more stable) energy, and a whole crystal lattice is more stable still.

What we observe

Salt (NaCl), hydrogen gas (H2), copper metal (Cu) (Day 7 p.14); white salt crystals, orange-brown bromine, and a spool of copper wire (Day 8 p.15). Ionic compounds form crystalline lattices: “dense, highly ordered structures … almost always solids” (Day 7 p.16).

What we write

Eel = 2.31 × 10−19 J·nm (Q1Q2/d) (Day 7 p.15). Lattice energies U (kJ/mol, Day 7 p.17): NaCl −786, KCl −720, MgO −3791. For a covalent bond: a potential-energy curve with its minimum at −436 kJ/mol and 74 pm for H–H (Day 8 p.11).

What the particles are doing

A cation and an anion attract, and the energy falls as they approach, until their electron clouds overlap and repulsion takes over: the bottom of the well is the bond (Day 7 p.15). Two H atoms do the same, but by sharing their electrons (Day 8 p.11). In a metal, the valence electrons form a “sea” that flows among all the atoms (Day 8 p.14).

Source: Day 7 p.12–17; Day 8 p.11, p.14–15.

How it responds The charge product usually dominates: a 2+/2− pair has Q1Q2 = −4, four times a 1+/1− pair at the same d. Then smaller ions (smaller d) give a stronger attraction.

Limits The equation describes the attraction only. The repulsive wall at very short distances (Day 7 p.15, curve d) isn't in it.

Lattices: “The ionic lattices are even more stable than we might expect from our two-body equation for Coulombic attractions” (Day 7 p.17). One Na+–Cl− pair, times NA, gives about −492 kJ/mol; the real U is −786 kJ/mol.

The textbook explains the extra stability: each ion interacts with several neighbors (§4.1, PDF p.183, printed 149).

A covalent bond's energy curve

“Potential energy during covalent H-H bond formation” (Day 8 p.11): far apart, two separate H atoms have zero energy. As they approach, attraction lowers the energy; at 74 pm the two electron clouds merge and the energy reaches its minimum, −436 kJ/mol; closer still, repulsion drives it steeply up. It's the same attraction-minimum-repulsion shape as the ion pair on Day 7 p.15, but the bond comes from a shared pair, not from two ions.

The slide doesn't name the two numbers. The textbook calls 74 pm the H–H bond length and 436 kJ/mol the bond energy, the energy needed to break a mole of these bonds (§4.1, PDF p.184; §4.6, PDF p.208). Bond lengths and energies for many bonds are in §4.6.

Metallic bonds

“Atoms in metallic solids are held together by a ‘sea’ of mobile electrons that flows freely among all the atoms in a piece of metal. In a block of copper metal, there is no experimental evidence for any two atoms being ‘bonded’. Each copper atom shares its valence electrons with ALL its neighbors. This begins to account for the conductivity of metals, but that's all we'll say here in Chapter 4” (Day 8 p.14).

The textbook's word valence, used by itself, means an element's capacity to form bonds (§4.1, PDF p.181). The lecture calls this the bonding capacity (Day 8 p.18), in §4.4, Lewis symbols.

Textbook preview (§4.1, PDF p.180–185, printed 146–151)

Greenhouse gases: the section's title topic. CO2 and CH4 let visible sunlight through but absorb the infrared radiation Earth gives off, trapping heat; atmospheric CO2 rose from about 315 to more than 400 ppm in 50 years. Why these molecules absorb IR is in §4.9, also a preview.

Recognize it

  • Ion charges and a distance → Eel (d in nm, keep the signs).
  • “Rank lattice energies” → charge product first, then size.
  • A metal + a nonmetal → ionic; nonmetals only → covalent; a metal alone → metallic.
  • An energy-vs.-distance curve for two atoms → the minimum gives the bond distance and bond energy.

Common mistakes

  • d in pm instead of nm (1000× error).
  • Dropping the charges' signs.
  • Forgetting Eel is per pair (× NA for per mole).
  • Reading 74 pm on the H–H curve as an atom's radius: it's the distance between the two nuclei.
