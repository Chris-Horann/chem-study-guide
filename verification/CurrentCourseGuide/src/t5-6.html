Learn

Two molecules can have the same formula and the same Lewis structure and still be different substances, because their atoms are arranged differently in three dimensions. The lecture's example: “Molecular geometries are clearly more complicated than Lewis structures. One example is the existence of chiral molecules, like R(−) and S(+) carvone” (Day 10 p.6). One carvone smells of spearmint; its mirror image smells of caraway.

What we observe

Spearmint tea and caraway-seed bread, the photos on Day 10 p.6, owe their smells to the two carvones, both C10H14O. Our noses tell them apart (textbook §5.1, PDF p.232).

What we write

A Lewis structure is flat: this one drawing of CHBrClF fits both of its mirror-image forms. To show which form you mean, draw wedges (bonds toward you) and dashes (bonds away), as the slide's carvone drawings do (Day 10 p.6).

What the particles are doing

An sp3 carbon points its four bonds at the corners of a tetrahedron. When all four groups differ, there are two arrangements, mirror images of each other, and no rotation turns one into the other, just as no rotation turns a left hand into a right hand.

Source: Day 10 p.6; textbook §5.1, PDF p.232 (printed 198) and §5.6, PDF p.255–257 (printed 221–223).

How to use it “Although several features within molecules can lead to chirality, the most common is the presence of a chiral carbon atom” (PDF p.257). Compare whole groups, not just the atom that touches the carbon: CH3 and CH2CH3 are different groups. “To decide whether an sp3-hybridized carbon atom is a stereocenter, we often have to look beyond the atoms bonded directly to it” (PDF p.259).

Limiting cases A carbon with two identical groups (a CH2, a CH3, two Br atoms) can't be a stereocenter. “Planar molecules cannot be chiral because they have superimposable mirror images” (Sample Ex. 5.6, PDF p.259).

The mirror-image test (Fig. 5.40, PDF p.257). Bromochlorofluoromethane, CHBrClF, used in airplane fire extinguishers, has four different atoms on its carbon; dibromochloromethane, CHBr2Cl, which can form when water supplies are purified with chlorine, has only three different ones. Reflect each in a mirror, then rotate the image about its C–H bond and try to lay it on the original. CHBr2Cl's image fits: it's achiral. CHBrClF's never does, because once H, C, and F line up, Br and Cl are in each other's places: it's chiral.

Carvone's stereocenter (PDF p.257–258). The circled carbon carries an H, a –C(CH3)=CH2 group, and two ring –CH2– groups. Those two differ because going around the ring one way leads to the C=C double bond and the other way to the C=O group: “That asymmetry in the ring is the reason that carvone is not superimposable on its mirror image.”

Optical activity (Fig. 5.39, PDF p.256–257). Enantiomers rotate plane-polarized light in opposite directions: a (+) enantiomer rotates the plane clockwise, a (−) enantiomer counterclockwise. Fig. 5.39 shows the measurement: the light passes through a polarizing filter, then through a solution of the sample, and the plane of oscillation comes out turned. Compounds that do this are optically active. A 50 : 50 mixture, a racemic mixture, “does not rotate plane-polarized light at all” (PDF p.260).

Chirality in nature (PDF p.260). Nearly all amino acids are chiral (alanine's two enantiomers are Fig. 5.41), and “the human body is a chiral environment.” As many as half of prescription drugs are chiral, and “typically, only one enantiomer of a chiral drug is active”: one enantiomer of albuterol widens the airways and the other narrows them, and dextromethorphan is a cough suppressant while its enantiomer, levomethorphan, is an opioid (PDF p.230). Laboratory syntheses often give racemic mixtures, so drug makers either make one enantiomer selectively or separate the two.

Molecular recognition (§5.1, PDF p.232; §5.5, PDF p.253) is why it matters: a molecule acts on a receptor only if their 3-D shapes are complementary. “Just as a left glove fits only a left hand, the spearmint molecule fits only the spearmint-shaped site, and the caraway molecule fits only the caraway-shaped site.” The textbook's other example, tomatoes whose ethylene receptors speed their ripening, opens §5.5 (PDF p.253).

Recognize it

  • An sp3 carbon with four different groups → a stereocenter → the molecule is chiral (the most common case).
  • Two identical groups on a carbon (CH2, CH3, two Br) → not a stereocenter.
  • Every carbon sp2 and the molecule flat → achiral.
  • A ring carbon → walk around the ring both ways; different paths make different groups.
  • A chiral compound that doesn't rotate polarized light → a racemic mixture.

One textbook sentence mixes two labels: “Our bodies can make proteins only from the (+) enantiomers of the 20 common amino acids” (PDF p.260). Proteins are built from the L enantiomers, an arrangement label, and the sign of rotation doesn't follow it (L-phenylalanine, for example, is (−) in water). Glycine, one of the 20, isn't chiral at all.

R and S, as in the slide's “R(−) and S(+) carvone,” describe the arrangement of the four groups around a stereocenter, assigned by naming rules from organic chemistry that neither the slides nor the textbook's §5.6 cover. (+) and (−) are measured with a polarimeter. Neither label predicts the other; the slide pairs them correctly: (R)-(−)-carvone is spearmint and (S)-(+)-carvone is caraway.

Common mistakes

  • Comparing only the atoms bonded directly to the carbon: CH3 and CH2CH3 are different groups.
  • Calling every tetrahedral molecule chiral: a carbon with two identical groups isn't a stereocenter.
  • Reading the R in “R(−)” as rotation to the right: (+) and (−) give the measured rotation, and R and S are a separate label.
  • Expecting a racemic mixture to rotate polarized light.
  • Thinking enantiomers differ in formula, bonds, or polarity.
