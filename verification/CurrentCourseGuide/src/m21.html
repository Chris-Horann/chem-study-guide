Learn

Lone pairs on the central atom count toward the steric number, so they help set the electron-pair geometry. But the molecular geometry describes the positions of the “atoms only” (Day 10 p.14), so a molecule with lone pairs gets a different shape name. Lone pairs also crowd the bonding pairs and shrink the bond angles.

What we observe

Measured bond angles: ozone 117° (Day 10 p.15), ammonia 107° (p.18), water 104.5° (p.19). Each is smaller than the ideal angle of its electron-pair geometry: 120°, 109.5°, and 109.5°.

What we write

Ozone's central O: 2 atoms + 1 lone pair. Ammonia's N: 3 + 1. Water's O: 2 + 2. The drawings are flat; the shapes come from the counts.

What the electrons are doing

The slide draws ozone's lone pair as a large lobe pressing on the two smaller bonding-pair lobes and squeezing them together (Day 10 p.15). The textbook's reason: bonding pairs are pulled toward two nuclei, while a lone pair stays close to the central atom and takes up more room there (PDF p.238).

Source: Day 10 p.14–19.

Understand

Electron-pair geometry: every region. Molecular geometry: atoms only.

SN
bonded atoms + lone pairs on the central atom; it sets the electron-pair geometry, one of the five arrangements of §5.1–5.2
shape
named from where the atoms are: “the molecular geometry describes relative positions of atoms only” (Day 10 p.14, p.16)

Angles Lone pairs squeeze the bond angles below the electron-pair geometry's ideal values: 120° → 117° in O3; 109.5° → 107° in NH3 → 104.5° in H2O (Day 10 p.15–19).

The lecture's three molecules

Ozone (Day 10 p.14–15): “SN = 3 with two atoms and one nonbonding pair predicts trigonal planar arrangement. Remember that the molecular geometry describes relative positions of atoms only. SN = 3 and two atoms predicts angular (“bent”) molecular geometry.” Both resonance structures give the same count, and the O–O–O angle is 117°.

Ammonia (Day 10 p.16–18): “SN = 4 with three atoms and one nonbonding pair predicts tetrahedral arrangement… SN = 4 and three atoms predicts trigonal pyramidal geometry,” with H–N–H = 107°. This settles a note hidden in the Day 8 p.23 file, “We'll see later that ammonia is tetrahedral”: tetrahedral is ammonia's electron-pair geometry, and its molecular geometry is trigonal pyramidal.

Water (Day 10 p.19): “SN = 4 with two atoms and two nonbonding pairs predicts tetrahedral arrangement. SN = 4 and two atoms predicts bent geometry. Note: the same geometry NAME as for ozone, but not the same bond angle!” Water's angle is 104.5°.

SN 5: axial or equatorial?

“Now things get more interesting.” Day 10 p.20 asks: “If we replace an atom in the trigonal bipyramid with a lone pair, will it occupy an axial or an equatorial position?” After a Top Hat slide (p.21; its poll isn't in the PDF), the answer slides put every lone pair in an equatorial position (p.22–23):

  • one lone pair → seesaw (shown after it's “Rotated 90° about horizontal axis,” p.22)
  • two lone pairs → T-shaped (p.23)
  • three lone pairs → linear: the two atoms are left in the axial positions, 180° apart (p.23)

SN 6: one lone pair, then two

“Weirdly, all of the positions are now equivalent again. It doesn't matter which position we replace with the lone pair!” → square pyramidal (Day 10 p.24). “But now consider a molecule with SN = 6 but with TWO lone pairs… Now things are again not equivalent!” The slide puts the two lone pairs opposite each other, 180° apart → square planar (Day 10 p.25).

Reading the table carefully:

  • It has no row for SN 5 with three lone pairs, although Day 10 p.23 shows that case: linear.
  • Its last row, SN 6 with three lone pairs (T-shaped), has no lecture figure. The textbook says of three or four lone pairs at SN 6, “Although these geometries are possible, we will not encounter any molecules with them” (PDF p.240). So “T-shaped” appears twice in the table, at SN 5 and at SN 6.
  • “See-saw” here, “Seesaw” in the p.22 caption and the textbook: the same shape.
  • The “ideal” angles are the electron-pair geometry's. With lone pairs the real angles are smaller: 107° and 104.5°, not 109.5° (p.18–19). The textbook's Table 5.1 writes these as “<109.5°” (PDF p.240).

Three lone pairs placed as far apart as possible on an octahedron leave the three atoms in a T, so the SN 6 row is a correct VSEPR prediction, just a rare one.

Textbook preview (§5.2, PDF p.237–243, printed 203–209)

Ranking repulsions (PDF p.238): “repulsion between lone pairs and bonding pairs is greater than repulsion between bonding pairs”; “repulsion caused by a lone pair is greater than repulsion caused by a double bond”; “repulsion caused by a double bond is greater than repulsion caused by a single bond”; and “two lone pairs of electrons on a central atom exert a greater repulsive force on the atom's bonding pairs than does one lone pair.” Hence CH4 109.5° > NH3 107.0° > H2O 104.5° (Sample Ex. 5.2, PDF p.238–239).

Why equatorial (PDF p.241): “the repulsions between pairs of electrons increase as the angle between them decreases.” An equatorial lone pair has two neighbors at 90° and an axial one would have three, so the textbook's figure labels the axial version “DOES NOT EXIST” (Fig. 5.13). Its formal name for the seesaw is disphenoidal.

SN 2 with a lone pair leaves only one other atom: a two-atom molecule, which is linear and has no bond angle (PDF p.237).

Table 5.1 (PDF p.240) starts at SN 3 and gives an example of each shape it lists. Besides the lecture's NH3 and H2O, its lone-pair examples are SO2 bent; SCl4 seesaw; BrF3 T-shaped; XeF2 linear; IF5 square pyramidal; XeF4 square planar. It writes the squeezed angles as “<120°,” “<109.5°,” and “<90°,” but gives XeF2 180° and XeF4 90°.

Distortions (PDF p.242): one lone pair at SN 6 closes the angles below 90° (“In BrF5, for example, the angles between its equatorial and axial bonds are 85°”), while two opposite lone pairs balance, so square planar angles stay exactly 90° and 180°.

Sample Ex. 5.3, SF4 (PDF p.242–243): SN 4 + 1 = 5, trigonal bipyramidal electron-pair geometry, seesaw molecular geometry; the equatorial lone pair closes the angles below 90°, 120°, and 180°.

Recognize it

  • A lone pair on the central atom → the electron-pair and molecular geometries have different names. Check which one the question asks for.
  • “Shape” means the molecular geometry: where the atoms are.
  • SN 5 with lone pairs → put them equatorial: seesaw, T-shaped, linear.
  • SN 6 with two lone pairs → opposite each other: square planar.
  • A bond angle next to lone pairs → expect it smaller than the table's ideal value (NH3 107°, H2O 104.5°, not 109.5°); more lone pairs, smaller angle. Lone pairs that sit symmetrically are the exception (preview box).

Common mistakes

  • Giving the electron-pair geometry when the shape is asked: NH3 is trigonal pyramidal, not tetrahedral.
  • Leaving the central atom's lone pairs out of the steric number.
  • Putting an SN 5 lone pair axial.
  • Putting two SN 6 lone pairs next to each other instead of opposite.
  • Quoting the table's ideal angle when lone pairs squeeze the bonds: NH3 is 107°, not 109.5°.
