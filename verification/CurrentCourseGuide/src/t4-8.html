Learn

The octet rule has three kinds of exceptions. Be, B, and Al form electron-deficient molecules with fewer than 8 electrons around them. Molecules with an odd number of electrons, free radicals, can't pair every electron. And nonmetals in period 3 and below can appear to hold more than an octet.

What we observe

Nitrogen monoxide from car exhaust reacts with O2 to form NO2, a key player in photochemical smog (PDF p.212). Free radicals like these are typically very reactive.

What we write

BeCl2 (Be has 4), NO (N has 7), SF6 (S has 12).

What the electrons are doing

Be has only two valence electrons to share. NO has 5 + 6 = 11 valence electrons, so one must stay unpaired. S in SF6 shares all six of its valence electrons, one with each F.

Source: textbook §4.8, PDF p.212–217 (printed 178–183). The professor has flagged it (“Sometimes it is impossible for every atom to have an octet,” Day 8 p.26) but hasn't taught it.

A period-2 atom can never exceed 8: nine electrons on N would be “impossible for an atom with only four orbitals in its valence shell” (PDF p.212). How period-3 atoms hold more is debated; the textbook says d orbitals “contribute little” and leaves the explanation to Chapter 5 (PDF p.214).

Recognize it

  • An odd valence-electron total → a free radical; give the octets to the more electronegative atoms.
  • Be, B, or Al as the central atom → expect fewer than 8.
  • P, S, Cl, or a heavier nonmetal with 5 or 6 bonds, or with O atoms whose formal charges could be lowered → an expanded octet may fit.
  • C, N, O, or F never exceeds 8.

Common mistakes

  • Forcing an octet with a multiple bond that puts a positive formal charge on F.
  • Giving a period-2 atom more than 8 electrons.
  • Leaving the more electronegative atom short in a free radical.
  • Assuming every period-3 atom must exceed an octet.
