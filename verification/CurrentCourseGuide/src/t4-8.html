Learn

“Not all atoms have a complete octet when forming covalent bonds” (Day 9 p.27). Hydrogen “forms duets,” and beyond hydrogen the slides give three kinds of exceptions: electron-deficient molecules of Be, B, and Al; species with an odd number of electrons, the “radicals” or “free radicals”; and expanded octets on nonmetals in the third row and below (Day 9 p.27–29).

What we observe

Radicals “are very reactive” (Day 9 p.28). Nitrogen monoxide and nitrogen dioxide, the slide's examples, each have an odd number of valence electrons, so one electron can't be paired.

What we write

BCl3 (B has 6), NO (N has 7), SF6 (S has 12), as drawn on Day 9 p.27–30.

What the electrons are doing

B has only three valence electrons to share, so it makes three bonds and stops at 6. NO has 5 + 6 = 11 valence electrons, so one stays unpaired. S in SF6 shares all six of its valence electrons, one with each F.

Source: Day 9 p.27–30.

When does an octet expand? (Day 9 p.29–30)

“Atoms will expand their octet when they bond with strongly electronegative elements (F, O, and Cl). An expanded shell produces a structure whose atoms' formal charges are closer to zero.” Sulfate is the worked example: with four S–O single bonds every atom has an octet, but S is +2 and each O −1. Now S is 0, the two double-bonded O atoms are 0, and the other two are −1. The sum is still −2, the ion's charge (rule 4, §4.7), and S holds 12 electrons. The same slide shows PCl5 and SF6 without formal-charge labels; work them out and every atom is 0.

The slides give no mechanism for expanded octets: hypervalency “is not well understood” (Day 9 p.29). Use the rule (row 3 or below, bonded to F, O, or Cl, formal charges closer to zero) without inventing a reason.

Textbook preview (§4.8, PDF p.212–217, printed 178–183)

Where the odd electron goes. The textbook leaves the incomplete octet on the less electronegative atom: giving N an octet in NO would leave O with 7, which “makes matters worse because now the more electronegative element (O) … has an incomplete octet” (PDF p.213). NO2's odd electron also lets two molecules pair up as N2O4 (Sample Ex. 4.17, PDF p.213–214).

The same rule, with a number. The textbook words it like the slide and adds the atomic numbers: nonmetals “in the third row and below in the periodic table (Z > 12)” (PDF p.214).

Why row 2 stops at 8. Nine electrons on N is “impossible for an atom with only four orbitals in its valence shell” (PDF p.212). Studies show that “d orbitals contribute little to the bonding” in expanded octets (PDF p.214).

Phosphate. With one P=O, P goes from +1 to 0, and the double bond can sit on any of the four O atoms (Sample Ex. 4.18, PDF p.215–216).

An average again. Experiments suggest the real bonding in SO42− and H2SO4 “is an average of the structures with an octet and the structures with more than an octet of valence electrons” (PDF p.215).

Another model. The textbook's §5.7 explains PCl5 and SF6 without expanding any octet, using bonds shared over three atoms (PDF p.271–273; see the textbook preview in §5.7).

Recognize it

  • An odd valence-electron total → a free radical: one electron stays unpaired.
  • Be, B, or Al as the central atom → expect fewer than 8 (4 for Be, 6 for B and Al).
  • P, S, Cl, or a heavier nonmetal at the center, bonded to F, O, or Cl, with more than four bonds or with formal charges an expansion would lower → an expanded octet.
  • C, N, O, and F never exceed 8: they're in row 2.

Common mistakes

  • Forcing an octet on B with a B=F or B=Cl bond, which puts a positive formal charge on the halogen.
  • Giving a row-2 atom more than 8 electrons.
  • Trying to pair every electron in a species with an odd total.
  • Expanding an octet that doesn't need it: the slide expands sulfate's because that lowers the formal charges.
