Learn

A Lewis structure shows which atoms are bonded, not how they sit in space. VSEPR theory gets the 3-D shape from the Lewis structure: the electron pairs around a central atom repel one another, so they spread out as far as they can. Count the regions of electron density around the central atom, its steric number, and the shape follows.

What we observe

Day 10 p.6 pairs spearmint and caraway with two forms of carvone, “chiral molecules” with the same atoms bonded the same way: “Molecular geometries are clearly more complicated than Lewis structures” (Day 10 p.6; more in §5.6). The two forms are why spearmint and caraway smell and taste different (textbook Fig. 5.1, PDF p.232). Measured bond angles are 180° in CO2 and 109.5° in CH4 (Day 10 p.7).

What we write

“Sometimes Lewis structures are enough…” (Day 10 p.7): the straight O=C=O matches CO2's 180° shape, but CH4's flat cross shows 90° angles the molecule doesn't have.

What the electrons are doing

Every bonding pair and lone pair on the central atom is a cloud of negative charge. The clouds repel and settle as far apart as possible. For CH4's four bonds that means pointing to the corners of a tetrahedron, 109.5° apart, not to the corners of a flat square (Day 10 p.8–11).

Day 10's “Representation Matters” slide features Lloyd Noel Ferguson, an organic chemist who “studied how three-dimensional structure affects biological activity,” including how very similar molecules can taste very different (Day 10 p.3).

Source: Day 10 p.3, p.6–11.

Understand

Steric number (SN) = the number of regions of electron density around the central atom

region
a lone pair on the central atom, or a bond to one neighboring atom, whether single, double, or triple
SN
“how many regions of high electron density surround the central atom / Or / In how many directions are there electrons?” (Day 10 p.9)

Also called the number of “electron domains” (the column heading on Day 10 p.26; “one hybridized orbital for every electron domain,” Day 11 p.12).

Why it works VSEPR “is based on the principle that electrons have negative charge and repel one another. It assumes that pairs of electrons are arranged about central atoms in ways that minimize repulsions between the pairs” (Day 10 p.8). Each region is one direction for a cloud of charge, so the number of regions decides the arrangement.

Route Lewis structure → SN → electron-pair geometry → molecular geometry. “To predict molecular geometry, you must know electron-pair geometry… and to know the electron-pair geometry, you must have a valid Lewis structure!” (Day 10 p.8)

Two geometries. The electron-pair geometry “describes the relative position in three-dimensional space of all the bonding and lone pairs of electrons”; the molecular geometry “describes the relative positions of the atoms in a molecule” (Day 10 p.8). When the central atom has no lone pairs, every region ends at an atom and the two have the same name. Lone pairs are the next module, §5.2, lone pairs.

Double bonds: one region, but a stronger push

“How do double bonds affect these predictions? Double bonds consist of MORE electrons than single bonds, so they repel the other electrons more strongly. Consider formaldehyde: What would you predict the H-C-H bond angle to be? It's about 118°” (Day 10 p.13).

So the C=O double bond still counts once (formaldehyde's C has SN 3 and is trigonal planar), but it squeezes the two C–H bonds a little closer than 120°.

The measured H–C–H angle is about 116.5°. The slide's “about 118°” and the textbook's “slightly smaller than the 120°” (below) agree on the direction, and the direction is the point: below 120°.

Textbook preview (§5.1–5.2, PDF p.232–237, printed 198–203)

Shape and recognition (§5.1): the spearmint and caraway molecules have “that same molecular formula and the same Lewis structure,” and each fits only its own site in our nasal membranes, a phenomenon called molecular recognition (PDF p.232). Lewis structures “don't show how the atoms are oriented in three dimensions” (PDF p.233).

Equation 5.1: SN = (number of atoms bonded to the central atom) + (number of lone pairs on the central atom) (PDF p.234). It gives the same count as the lecture's regions, because a double or triple bond connects just one atom.

Three steps (PDF p.235): (1) draw the Lewis structure; (2) determine the steric number of the central atom; (3) use the steric number to predict the electron-pair and molecular geometries.

Axial and equatorial: the three equatorial bonds of a trigonal bipyramid are 120° apart, each axial bond is 90° from them, and the two axial bonds are 180° apart. The six positions of an octahedron are equivalent, arranged “just like the axes of an xyz coordinate system” (PDF p.234).

Drawing in 3-D: a solid wedge is a bond coming out of the page toward you, a dashed wedge a bond going into the page, and a plain line a bond in the plane of the page (PDF p.236).

Balloons: two to six balloons tied tightly together push one another into the same five arrangements (Fig. 5.4, PDF p.235).

Formaldehyde: the H–C–H angle “is slightly smaller than the 120° predicted for a trigonal planar geometry,” and the two H–C=O angles are larger to make up for it. “VSEPR does not enable us to predict the actual values of those bond angles, but it does allow us to correctly predict that bond angles deviate from the ideal values of an idealized trigonal planar molecule” (PDF p.237).

BF3 is drawn as “The Lewis structure of BF3 that results in zero formal charges on all atoms” (PDF p.236): three single bonds, six electrons on B. A Concept Test asks which steric numbers go with fewer than, exactly, or more than an octet when every bond is single (PDF p.235).

Recognize it

  • “Predict the shape,” “the bond angle,” or “the electron-pair geometry” → draw the Lewis structure, then count regions on the central atom.
  • Each bond counts once, whatever its order; each lone pair on the central atom counts once.
  • No lone pairs on the central atom → the electron-pair and molecular geometries have the same name.
  • Five neighbors → two kinds of position (axial and equatorial); six → all alike.
  • A double bond next to single bonds → the angles between the single bonds close up a little.

Common mistakes

  • Counting a double or triple bond as two or three regions.
  • Counting electrons instead of regions (BF3's six, SF6's twelve).
  • Reading angles off the flat Lewis drawing: CH4's cross looks like 90°.
  • Skipping the Lewis structure: “you must have a valid Lewis structure!” (Day 10 p.8).
