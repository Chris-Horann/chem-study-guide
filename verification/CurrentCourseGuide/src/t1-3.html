Learn

Everything with mass that takes up space is matter, and the textbook sorts it with two questions. First: can a physical process separate it? If yes, it's a mixture; if no, it's a pure substance. Second, for a pure substance: can a chemical reaction break it down? If yes, it's a compound; if no, it's an element. Mixtures are homogeneous (uniform throughout) or heterogeneous (distinct regions).

What we observe

Gold melts at 1064 °C and is still gold: a physical property. Iron rusts into a new substance: a chemical property. Oil and water separate into layers (heterogeneous); salt water looks the same everywhere (homogeneous).

What we write

Density, d = m/V (textbook Eq. 1.1), is an intensive property: the same for any amount. Mass and volume are extensive: they grow with the sample.

What the particles are doing

An element's particles are all one kind of atom (single or bonded, like O2). A compound's particles contain two or more kinds of atom in a fixed ratio. A mixture's particles are different substances mingled in any proportion.

d = m/V

d
density (g/cm3 or g/mL; 1 mL = 1 cm3)
m
mass (g)
V
volume (cm3 or mL)

Use it when you relate the mass and volume of a substance, or identify a substance by its density. Density is intensive, so it identifies pure substances.

Rearranged m = dV and V = m/d. Density works as a conversion factor between mass and volume (§1.8).

Separating mixtures uses physical differences

MethodExploits a difference in…Particle-level reason
distillationvolatilityParticles that attract each other weakly escape into the vapor more easily; the vapor is collected and condensed.
filtrationparticle sizeSolid particles larger than the filter's pores are trapped; dissolved ions and water pass through.
chromatographyaffinity for a stationary vs. a mobile phaseComponents that interact more strongly with the stationary phase move more slowly.

Recognize it

  • “Can be separated by filtering, evaporating, distilling” → mixture.
  • “Decomposes when heated or electrolyzed into simpler substances” → compound.
  • “Observed without changing the substance” (color, melting point, density) → physical property; “reacts with…” → chemical property.

Constant composition (Day 1 p.11, covered in lecture) is what separates a compound from a homogeneous mixture: a compound's composition can't vary.

Common mistakes

  • Calling a clear solution a compound because it looks uniform.
  • Calling melting or boiling a chemical change. The substance's identity doesn't change.
  • Treating density as extensive. A small chip of gold has the same density as a bar.
