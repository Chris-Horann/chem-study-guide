Learn

To write an ion's configuration, start from the neutral atom. Anions add electrons by the usual rules. Cations lose electrons from the highest principal quantum number first, “regardless of the order in which they were added” (Day 6 p.21). For transition metals, that means 4s empties before 3d.

What we observe

Main-group ions end up with noble-gas configurations: F− and Na+ both equal [Ne] (Day 6 p.21).

What we write

F + e− → F−: [He]2s22p5 → [He]2s22p6 = [Ne]. Na → Na+ + e−: [Ne]3s1 → [Ne]. BUT Ni → Ni2+ + 2e−: [Ar]3d84s2 → [Ar]3d8 (Day 6 p.21).

What the electrons are doing

The highest-n electrons are the outermost and leave first. For Ni that's the two 4s electrons, even though 4s filled before 3d.

Source: Day 6 p.21–23.

Understand

electrons in an ion = Z − charge

Anion Continue Aufbau with the extra electrons: F− → [He]2s22p6 = [Ne].

Cation Write the neutral atom, then remove from the highest n; within a shell, p before s. Fe3+: [Ar]4s23d6 → remove 4s2, then one 3d → [Ar]3d5.

Isoelectronic Species with the same electron count, such as O2−, F−, Na+, Mg2+, and Al3+ (all 10) (§3.10).

The textbook's reason 4s electrons ionize first: as 3d fills, 3d electrons feel a larger Zeff than 4s electrons, so 4s ends up higher in energy (textbook §3.9, PDF p.154–155, printed 120–121). It also notes that transition metals often form 2+ ions by losing both s electrons.

Recognize it

  • A transition-metal cation → 4s leaves first.
  • A main-group ion → it usually lands on a noble-gas configuration.
  • “How many d electrons in V3+?” → the neutral atom, then remove 4s, then 3d.
Lecture signal“regardless of the order in which they were added” and the 4s² are printed bold (Day 6 p.21); a Top Hat question asks for V3+'s 3d electrons (Day 6 p.23, in Practice below).

Common mistakes

  • Removing 3d electrons first (“last in, first out”).
  • Adding electrons for a positive charge.
  • Forgetting that anions keep filling by Aufbau.
