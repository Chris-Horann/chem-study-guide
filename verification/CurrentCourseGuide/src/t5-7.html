Learn

Liquid oxygen sticks to a magnet, and “Only molecules with unpaired electrons are paramagnetic” (Day 11 p.27, repeated on Day 12 p.6). Yet the Lewis structure O=O pairs every electron, and the models built on it can't do better: “Lewis structures, VSEPR, and valence bond/hybridization theories ALL fail to predict this.” Molecular orbital (MO) theory explains it. It takes “ALL the valence atomic orbitals from the individual atoms” and spreads them over the whole molecule (Day 12 p.7), and O2's last two electrons land, unpaired, in two orbitals of equal energy (Day 12 p.11).

What we observe

Liquid O2 poured between the poles of a magnet hangs there, held by the field (the photo on Day 11 p.27 and Day 12 p.6; textbook Fig. 5.51, PDF p.267). Oxygen is paramagnetic: it is attracted to a magnetic field.

What we write

The Lewis structure (Day 8 p.20), with every electron paired, and O2's MO diagram (Day 12 p.11), which the textbook writes as a configuration (PDF p.265): (σ2s)2(σ*2s)2(σ2p)2(π2p)4(π*2p)2, with two unpaired electrons.

What the electrons are doing

O2's 12 valence electrons fill molecular orbitals from the lowest energy up. The last two go into a pair of π*2p orbitals of equal energy, one in each, with parallel spins: Hund's rule, as for carbon's two 2p electrons (Day 6 p.16). Day 12 p.11 circles those two electrons in red.

Source: Day 11 p.27; Day 12 p.6–11; Day 8 p.20; Day 6 p.16; textbook §5.7, PDF p.265–267 (printed 231–233).

Connected lecture ideas

Hund's rule: “maximize unpaired spins when putting electrons into degenerate orbitals” (Day 6 p.16). Free radicals such as NO and NO2 have an odd number of electrons, so some are unpaired (Day 9 p.28). A higher bond order means a shorter, stronger bond (Table 4.6, Day 9 p.14). Ozone's resonance hybrid has five dots on each end O (Day 9 p.10). PCl5 and SF6 have expanded octets (Day 9 p.29–30). σ bonds have their electron density along the bond axis and π bonds above and below it (Day 11 p.10, p.17).

Meaning. The formula is on Day 12 p.8–9. It is the MO version of the number of bonds between the two atoms: Day 12 p.11 prints 1, 0, 1, 2, 3, 2, 1, 0 for Li2 through Ne2. The textbook adds that “the strength of the bond and the stability of the molecule both increase as the bond order increases” (PDF p.263), the trend Table 4.6 shows from single to triple bonds (Day 9 p.14).

Limiting cases. Bond order 0 means no net bond: He2 (Day 12 p.9), Be2, and Ne2 (p.11). The textbook says He2 “does not exist” (PDF p.262). Half-integer values are allowed: H2− (Day 12 p.9) has ½(2 − 1) = ½, as the textbook's Sample Ex. 5.7 finds (PDF p.263).

Count only valence electrons: only “the valence atomic orbitals” go into the MOs (Day 12 p.7). The textbook: “core electrons do not participate in bonding” (PDF p.264).

MOs from the 2s and 2p orbitals (Day 12 p.10–11)

Each period-2 atom brings a 2s and three 2p orbitals. The two 2s orbitals give σ2s and σ*2s (drawn on Day 12 p.11). The two 2p orbitals that point along the bond axis (2pz) give σ2p and σ*2p; the two pairs perpendicular to it (2px and 2py) give two π2p and two π*2p MOs, with lobes above and below, or in front of and behind, the axis (Day 12 p.10, the textbook's Fig. 5.48). Eight valence atomic orbitals in, eight MOs out.

HOMO and LUMO (Day 12 p.11)

Day 12 p.11 also names two orbitals: “HOMO: Highest Occupied Molecular Orbital” and “LUMO: Lowest Unoccupied Molecular Orbital.” Read off the slide's diagrams, N2's HOMO is σ2p and its LUMO the π*2p pair; O2's HOMO is the half-filled π*2p pair and its LUMO σ*2p.

Outcome 4 (Day 12 p.5) includes UV/visible spectra. A molecule that absorbs light moves an electron up to a higher MO (textbook PDF p.261), and the smallest such jump is from the HOMO to the LUMO. The slides give the two definitions without an example; the Top Hat question that follows (p.12) isn't in the PDF.

Recognize it

  • “Paramagnetic,” “attracted to a magnet,” or “unpaired electrons” for a diatomic → fill its MO diagram (Day 12 p.11).
  • The bond order of a diatomic molecule or ion → count the valence electrons (one more per unit of negative charge, one fewer per unit of positive charge), fill from the bottom, then ½(bonding − antibonding) (Day 12 p.8–9).
  • Which order? Li2 through N2: π2p below σ2p. O2 through Ne2: σ2p below π2p (Day 12 p.11). The textbook uses the O2 order for NO and fills an ion with its parent molecule's order (preview below).
  • Bond order 0 → no net bond, so the molecule isn't expected to exist (He2, Be2, Ne2; Day 12 p.9, p.11).
  • Highest filled or lowest empty MO → HOMO or LUMO (Day 12 p.11).
  • A magnetic or spectroscopic question → MO theory; connectivity → Lewis structures; 3-D shape → VSEPR or valence bond theory (Day 12 p.13).
Textbook preview (§5.7 Molecular Orbital Theory, PDF p.260–273, printed 226–239)

Definitions. The textbook defines MO theory as “a bonding theory based on the mixing of atomic orbitals of similar shapes and energies to form molecular orbitals that belong to the molecule as a whole” (PDF p.260). Bonding MOs have “lobes of high electron density that lie between bonded pairs of atoms” and stabilize the molecule; antibonding MOs lie higher in energy and destabilize it (PDF p.261).

The five guidelines for building a diagram (PDF p.263–264). The slides state the first (Day 12 p.7) and fill their diagrams by the last two (Day 12 p.9, p.11):

  1. The number of molecular orbitals equals the number of atomic orbitals used to create them.
  2. Atomic orbitals with similar energy and orientation mix more effectively than do those with different energies and orientations.
  3. Better mixing leads to a larger energy difference between bonding and antibonding orbitals and thus greater stabilization of the bonding MOs.
  4. A molecular orbital can accommodate a maximum of two electrons; two electrons in the same MO have opposite spins.
  5. Electrons in ground-state molecules occupy the lowest-energy molecular orbitals available, following the aufbau principle and Hund's rule.

Why the order switches (Fig. 5.49, PDF p.265–266). The energy gap between 2s and 2p grows with atomic number. For Li2 through N2 (Z ≤ 7) it is small enough for some mixing of 2s and 2p, which “raises the energy of the σ2p orbital—enough to put it above the π2p orbital” (PDF p.266).

Magnetism (PDF p.266). The textbook names the opposite case: a substance whose molecules have every electron paired is diamagnetic, weakly repelled by a magnetic field. “The more unpaired electrons in a molecule, the greater the paramagnetism. Only MO theory accounts for the magnetic behavior of oxygen and other molecular substances.”

Ions (Sample Ex. 5.8, PDF p.267). An ion is filled with its parent molecule's order: add or remove electrons at the top of the diagram. Removing an antibonding electron raises the bond order (O2 2 → O2+ 2.5); removing a bonding one lowers it (N2 3 → N2+ 2.5).

Two different atoms: NO (Fig. 5.52, PDF p.268–269). O's atomic orbitals lie lower in energy than N's. Bonding MOs end up closer to the more electronegative atom's orbitals (O) and antibonding MOs closer to the less electronegative atom's (N), with σ2p below π2p, the O2 order. NO's 11 valence electrons leave one in π*2p: bond order ½(8 − 3) = 2.5, “halfway between the bond orders for N=O and N≡O,” which fits its 115 pm bond, halfway between N=O (122 pm) and N≡O (106 pm). The odd electron sits mostly on N, as in the Lewis structure: NO+ has bond order 3 and NO− 2 (Sample Ex. 5.9).

NO is the textbook's only two-element diagram. For some other two-element molecules, such as CO and CN−, detailed calculations put π2p below σ2p, as in N2. The bond orders and magnetism this section's problems ask for come out the same with either order.

Colors of auroras (Table 5.4, PDF p.269–270). Electrons can jump between MOs, absorbing or emitting UV or visible light, just as electrons in atoms do (the line spectra on Day 3 p.8–10). Excited N2 emits deep red light (650–680 nm), excited N2+ blue-violet (391–470 nm), and excited O atoms red (630 nm) and green (558 nm).

Ozone without resonance (Figs. 5.54–5.55, PDF p.270–271). Start from two O–O σ bonds and five lone pairs (14 electrons). The other 4 go into three MOs made from one p orbital on each O: a bonding π, a nonbonding n, and an antibonding π*. With π2n2, one π bond is spread over the whole molecule, so the average bond order is (2 + 1)/2 = 1.5, and the n pair is shared by the two end atoms, giving each 2.5 lone pairs on average. That is the professor's five-dot resonance hybrid (Day 9 p.10).

More than four electron pairs (“A Bonding Theory for SN > 4,” PDF p.271–273). The textbook explains XeF2, PCl5, and SF6 “without expanding their octets.” In XeF2, one 5p orbital of Xe and a 2p orbital of each F form three MOs (σ, n, σ*); four electrons fill σ and n, a bond order of 1 shared by two Xe–F bonds, ½ each. In PCl5 the three equatorial P–Cl bonds have bond order 1 (202 pm) and the two axial ones ½ (214 pm). SF6's six equal S–F bonds average [(4 × ½) + (2 × 1)]/6 = ⅔. The lecture draws these molecules with expanded octets, “hypervalency, and it is not well understood” (Day 9 p.29). Both are models of the same molecules; for this course's Lewis structures, use the lecture's expanded octets.

Textbook slips in §5.7. Sample Ex. 5.9 prints NO− as (σ2s)2(σ*2s)2(σ2s)2(π2p)4(π*2p)2; the third term should be (σ2p)2 (PDF p.269). The aurora text says N2+ lost “the other π*2p electron,” but ground-state N2 has no π* electrons, and the book's own Fig. 5.53 removes a σ2p electron (PDF p.270). Figs. 5.58(a) and 5.59(a) draw unhybridized P and S with one 3s electron instead of two (PDF p.272–273). Fig. 5.49's caption applies the O2 order to elements “beyond oxygen (Z ≥ 8)”; the text, and Day 12 p.11, use it for O2 itself (PDF p.265).

Common mistakes

  • Pairing O2's two π*2p electrons in one orbital instead of spreading them out (Hund's rule; the red circle on Day 12 p.11).
  • Counting the 1s core electrons of a period-2 diatomic: only valence orbitals go in (Day 12 p.7).
  • Forgetting the ½ in the bond-order formula.
  • Using one energy order for every molecule: B2 and C2 need π2p below σ2p (Day 12 p.11), or their magnetism comes out wrong.
  • Assuming that removing an electron always weakens a bond: O2+ has a higher bond order than O2.
