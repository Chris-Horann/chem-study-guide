Learn

Lewis structures and VSEPR say where the electron pairs are. Valence bond theory says which orbitals hold them: a covalent bond forms when half-filled orbitals on two atoms overlap (Day 11 p.9). To point its bonds the way VSEPR predicts, an atom first mixes (“averages”) its valence s and p orbitals into hybrid orbitals, one for every electron domain: “the number of hybridized orbitals equals the steric number of the atom” (Day 11 p.12).

What we observe

Methane's four C–H bonds are equivalent (Day 11 p.11), and every H–C–H angle is 109.5° (Day 10 p.7). Yet a carbon atom in its ground state has only two unpaired electrons, in 2p orbitals at right angles to each other (Day 11 p.11).

What the electrons are doing

Each of carbon's four sp3 hybrids holds one electron and points to a corner of a tetrahedron. Each overlaps head-on with an H 1s orbital, and the shared pair sits between the two nuclei: a σ bond (Day 11 p.10, p.15).

Understand

Valence bond theory (Day 11 p.9–10)

“VSEPR grew out of Lewis structures, which pre-date our understanding of atomic orbitals. Can we reconcile the two ideas? Valence bond theory (Linus Pauling, late 1920s) suggests that covalent bonds form when half-filled orbitals on different atoms overlap. The electrons in overlapping orbitals are attracted to the nuclei of both bonded atoms, increasing stability” (Day 11 p.9). The slide pairs this with the H–H energy curve from Day 8 p.11: as two H atoms approach, their half-filled 1s orbitals overlap, and the energy is lowest, −436 kJ/mol, with the nuclei 74 pm apart.

That overlap “leads to a sigma (σ) bond: A covalent bond in which the highest electron density lies between the two atoms along the bond axis” (Day 11 p.10).

“excited state” (promotion, rejected)

“But we pretty quickly run into trouble with this picture. Consider methane. Methane only has two unpaired electrons, so how can it make four sigma bonds? One suggestion is that it could promote an electron from 2s to 2p, producing four unpaired electrons. But that's not what methane looks like! We need four equivalent bonds pointing to the vertices of a tetrahedron!” (Day 11 p.11).

Promotion fixes the count but not the shape. Its four bonds would come from one spherical 2s orbital and three 2p orbitals at 90° to one another: one bond of one kind, three of another, three of them at right angles. Methane's four bonds are identical and 109.5° apart.

“Methane only has two unpaired electrons” means the carbon atom in its ground state, 2s22p2, as the slide's “Ground state” diagram shows. The CH4 molecule itself has no unpaired electrons.

Hybridization (Day 11 p.12–16)

“Pauling got around this by suggesting the idea of hybridization: The mixing of atomic orbitals to generate new sets of orbitals that are then available to form covalent bonds with other atoms. Because orbitals are “just” mathematical functions, you can do anything to them that you can do to any other math function – such as averaging them. We will need one hybridized orbital for every electron domain. That is, the number of hybridized orbitals equals the steric number of the atom” (Day 11 p.12).

number of hybrid orbitals = steric number

steric number (SN)
bonded atoms + lone pairs on the atom; “In how many directions are there electrons?” (Day 10 p.9). A double or triple bond counts once: CO2 has SN 2 (Day 10 p.10).
hybrid orbital
one of the new, equivalent orbitals made by mixing. Each has a large “Major lobe” and a small “Minor lobe” (Day 11 p.14); drawings usually show only the major lobe.
sp, sp2, sp3
SN 2, 3, 4 (Table 5.3, Day 11 p.24). The name records what was mixed: sp3 is one s and three p orbitals (textbook definitions, PDF p.249).

Orbitals in = hybrids out. “The number of valence atomic orbitals combined equals the number of hybrid orbitals created” (Day 11 p.20): SN 2 mixes the 2s and one 2p, SN 3 the 2s and two 2p, SN 4 the 2s and all three 2p.

Leftovers. A second-period atom has four valence orbitals. Whatever isn't mixed stays as unhybridized p orbitals: none for sp3, one for sp2, two for sp. They make π bonds (§5.4–5.5, next).

Electrons. Hybridizing rearranges the atom's own valence electrons; it doesn't add or remove any. Fill the hybrids singly first, pairing only when you must (the slides' box diagrams, Day 11 p.13, p.16).

Limits. H doesn't hybridize: “Hydrogen uses a 1s orbital to make bonds” (Day 11 p.20). The slides' Table 5.3 stops at sp3: no hybridization is given for SN 5 or 6.

The rules (Day 11 p.20)

“Hybrid orbital theory accounts for molecular geometries and bonding.”

  • “Each electron domain on the central atom requires one hybrid orbital.”
  • “The number of valence atomic orbitals combined equals the number of hybrid orbitals created.”
  • “σ bonds involve head-on overlap of hybrid orbitals. Exception: Hydrogen uses a 1s orbital to make bonds”
  • “π bonds always result from side-to-side overlap of unhybridized orbitals.”
  • “Lone pairs always reside in hybrid orbitals.”

Recognize it

  • “What is the hybridization of …?” → Lewis structure → count bonded atoms + lone pairs (a multiple bond once) → SN 2, 3, 4 → sp, sp2, sp3.
  • “Which orbitals overlap to form this bond?” → for a σ bond, a hybrid on each atom, or H's 1s; for a π bond, unhybridized p orbitals.
  • “Where is the lone pair?” → always in a hybrid orbital.
  • Three names, three questions: sp3 (hybridization), tetrahedral (electron-pair geometry), trigonal pyramidal (molecular geometry) all describe NH3's N.

Textbook preview (§5.4, PDF p.247–253, printed 213–219)

Same picture, no promotion. The textbook never promotes an electron. It mixes carbon's filled 2s and half-filled 2p orbitals directly, “a mathematical mixing of the wave functions” (PDF p.247), into four sp3 hybrids with one electron each (Fig. 5.23, PDF p.248). The professor's diagram on Day 11 p.13 does the same; promotion appears only on p.11, to be rejected.

Same counting rule. The steric number “always indicates the number of hybrid orbitals that must be created” (PDF p.251), and “the SN of an atom with an octet of valence electrons is directly linked to both its hybridization and its molecular geometry” (PDF p.248).

Drawn lobes. Every sp3 orbital has a major and a minor lobe, but “the minor lobe is not involved in bonding,” so the book leaves it out and stretches the lobes “to better show their orientation” (PDF p.248).

Energies. The textbook also ranks the energies of sp2 and sp3 hybrids on the same atom, reasoning from how many 2p orbitals go into each set (PDF p.249). Before you look it up, decide which set lies lower from the slides' diagrams, where 2s sits below 2p (Day 11 p.13, p.18); Practice asks.

Sample Exercise 5.5, CO2 (PDF p.251–252): the C is sp, with two σ bonds and two π bonds; each O is sp2, keeping one p orbital for its π bond (more in §5.4–5.5).

SN 5 and 6. The textbook's Table 5.3 also stops at sp3 (PDF p.252). It sets aside the older explanation that used “valence-shell d atomic orbitals to form hybrid orbitals” and treats PCl5 and SF6 with a mix of hybrid orbitals and three-center molecular orbitals instead (PDF p.271–273; §5.7).

Common mistakes

  • Counting only the bonded atoms: NH3's N has SN 4, not 3, because the lone pair is a domain.
  • Counting a double or triple bond as two or three domains.
  • Giving H a hybrid orbital; H bonds with its 1s.
  • Putting a lone pair in an unhybridized p orbital.
  • Reading Table 5.3's “Trigonal planar” row on Day 11 p.24 literally for sp3 with three σ bonds.
