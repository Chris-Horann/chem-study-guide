Learn

The more electron pairs two atoms share, the shorter and stronger their bond: Table 4.6 on Day 9 p.14 shows it in three of its four red-boxed groups (the fourth holds single bonds only), and outcome 7 asks you to “Describe how bond order, bond energy, and bond length are related” (Day 9 p.5). When resonance averages a single and a double bond, the bond's length falls in between (Day 9 p.7).

What we observe

Measured bond lengths and bond energies, averaged over many compounds: C–C 154 pm and 348 kJ/mol, C=C 134 pm and 614 kJ/mol, C≡C 120 pm and 839 kJ/mol (Table 4.6, Day 9 p.14). Ozone's two bonds: 128 pm each (Day 9 p.7).

What we write

–, =, ≡ in the table's Bond column: one, two, or three shared pairs. The table's columns are “Bond Length (pm)” and “Bond Energy (kJ/mol)” (Day 9 p.14).

What the electrons are doing

Each extra shared pair adds electron density between the two nuclei, which pulls them closer and holds them more tightly. (The slides show the numbers; this explanation is ours.)

Source: Day 9 p.5, p.7, p.14, with Day 8 p.11.

Understand

Reading the red boxes (Table 4.6, Day 9 p.14)

aThe table's footnote gives 799 kJ/mol for the carbon–oxygen bonds in CO2.

Strength doesn't add up. A double bond is stronger than a single bond, but not twice as strong: 614 kJ/mol for C=C against 2 × 348 = 696, and 839 kJ/mol for C≡C against 3 × 348 = 1044.

Ozone is the lecture's in-between case: one O–O and one O=O in any single drawing, but two identical 128 pm bonds in the molecule, between 148 and 121 pm. That intermediate length is the evidence for resonance (Day 9 p.7, §4.5).

Counting shared pairs turns the lecture's “average of the two” (Day 9 p.9) into a number: a bond that is single (1 pair) in one structure and double (2 pairs) in the other averages 1.5 pairs. The slides give no such number; the textbook calls it the bond order (preview below).

Connected lecture ideas

The H–H energy curve (Day 8 p.11) is a single bond's picture: its minimum, −436 kJ/mol at 74 pm, matches Table 4.6's H–H row, 74 pm and 436 kJ/mol (Day 9 p.14). The bond length is where the curve bottoms out, and the bond energy is the depth of the well, listed as a positive number. Atomic size grows down a group (Day 7 p.7), which is why the halogen bonds get longer.

Textbook preview (§4.6, PDF p.206–208, printed 172–174; the slides number it §4.5)

Definitions. The slides name bond order, bond energy, and bond length only in outcome 7 and the table's headings. The textbook: bond order is the number of bonds between two atoms, 1 for a single bond, 2 for a double, 3 for a triple (PDF p.206); bond energy is “the energy needed to break one mole of bonds in the gas phase,” and it's always positive because breaking a bond takes energy (PDF p.208).

Fractional bond orders. Averaging over resonance structures gives bond orders between whole numbers: ozone 1.5 (PDF p.206); carbonate 4 bonding pairs over 3 bonds = 4/3 ≈ 1.33, so its C–O bonds should fall between 143 and 123 pm; measured, 129 pm (Sample Ex. 4.15, PDF p.207–208). “Bond length, then, can be used to determine bond order, and vice versa” (PDF p.206).

Averages vary. C=O is 799 kJ/mol in CO2 but 743 kJ/mol in formaldehyde (PDF p.208), which is why the table's values are averages.

Recognize it

  • “Which bond is shorter or stronger?” for the same two atoms → compare bond orders first.
  • Different atoms, all single bonds → atom size sets the length (the halogen box).
  • A measured length between a single and a double value → an averaged, resonance bond.
  • “Estimate from Table 4.6” → the values are averages, so the answer is approximate.

Common mistakes

  • Averaging 1 and 2 no matter how many bonds share the extra pair (the textbook's carbonate is 4/3, not 1.5).
  • Expecting a double bond to be twice as strong as a single bond.
  • Mixing up “longer” and “stronger”: for one pair of atoms they run in opposite directions.
  • Expecting bond energy to fall smoothly down a group (F–F is weaker than Cl–Cl).
