Learn

“Each double bond consists of a sigma bond AND a pi (π) bond” (Day 11 p.17), and a triple bond is one σ bond and two π bonds. σ bonds come from head-on overlap of hybrid orbitals (or H's 1s); π bonds come from side-to-side overlap of the unhybridized p orbitals left over after hybridizing, with their electron density above and below the bond axis (Day 11 p.20). In a molecule with several “central” atoms, work out each atom's steric number and hybridization in turn.

What we observe

Day 11 p.25 marks both carbons of ethylene, C2H4, “Trigonal planar.” Its C=C bond is shorter and stronger than a C–C single bond: 134 pm and 614 kJ/mol versus 154 pm and 348 kJ/mol (Table 4.6, Day 9 p.14).

What we write

One line of each double bond is σ and the other is π. Day 11 p.19 colors formaldehyde's σ bond lines green and its π bond line blue.

What the electrons are doing

Each carbon in ethylene uses three sp2 hybrids for σ bonds (to two H atoms and the other C) and keeps one 2p orbital perpendicular to the plane of those three bonds. When the two carbons' 2p orbitals are parallel, they overlap side by side, above and below the C–C axis: one π bond with two lobes (Day 11 p.25).

Source: Day 11 p.17–20, p.25; Day 9 p.14.

Understand

“Pauling was also able to explain those mysterious double bonds in our Lewis structures. Each double bond consists of a sigma bond AND a pi (π) bond: A covalent bond in which electron density is greatest above and below the bonding axis. To illustrate, let's look at formaldehyde” (Day 11 p.17).

single bond = 1 σ · double bond = 1 σ + 1 π · triple bond = 1 σ + 2 π

σ bond
highest electron density between the two atoms, along the bond axis (Day 11 p.10); head-on overlap of hybrid orbitals, or of H's 1s (p.20)
π bond
electron density greatest above and below the bonding axis (Day 11 p.17); side-to-side overlap of unhybridized orbitals (p.20)
unhybridized p orbital
a valence p orbital left out of the hybrid set: one on an sp2 atom (“One unhybridized p orbital,” Day 11 p.18), two on an sp atom (“Two unhybridized p orbitals,” p.23)

Counting. σ bonds = the number of bonded pairs of atoms: every connection has exactly one, including each bond to H. π bonds = the extra lines: one per double bond, two per triple bond.

Hybridization limits π. An sp3 atom has no p orbital left, so it makes no π bonds; an sp2 atom can make one; an sp atom two (a triple bond, or two double bonds).

Several centers. Treat each atom other than H as its own center: count its bonded atoms and lone pairs → SN → hybridization, as the lecture does for both N atoms of diazene (Day 11 p.22) and both C atoms of ethylene (p.25).

C has three electron domains (two H atoms and the O), so it makes three sp2 hybrids and keeps one p orbital. O has one bonded atom and two lone pairs, also SN 3, so it's sp2 too. The σ bonds: two H 1s–C sp2 and one C sp2–O sp2. The π bond: the C and O p orbitals, side by side, above and below the molecular plane. O's lone pairs sit in its other two sp2 hybrids (Day 11 p.19). The extra electrons of the double bond are why VSEPR puts the H–C–H angle at “about 118°” rather than 120° (Day 10 p.13).

Recognize it

  • “How many σ and π bonds?” → σ = bonded pairs of atoms (count every C–H); π = the extra lines of double and triple bonds.
  • For a C, N, or O atom with an octet: only single bonds and lone pairs → sp3; one double bond → sp2; a triple bond or two double bonds → sp.
  • “Four bonds” on a carbon is a trap: count electron domains, not bond lines. Ethylene's carbons have four bond lines but SN 3.
  • Several centers → go atom by atom; one molecule can contain sp3, sp2, and sp atoms side by side.

One bond, two lobes. “Note that the π bond is one bond formed by the two atoms' p orbitals through two points of contact—it is not two bonds” (PDF p.249).

N2 (Fig. 5.30, PDF p.251): each N is sp. “A lone pair occupies one of the two sp orbitals on each nitrogen atom, and the other sp hybrid orbital forms the σ bond”; the triple bond is one σ and two π.

CO2 (Sample Ex. 5.5, PDF p.251–252): the sp carbon makes two σ bonds with its sp hybrids and two π bonds with its two p orbitals, one to each O. Each O is sp2. Because carbon's two p orbitals are 90° apart, the two π bonds are perpendicular: one above and below the molecular plane, the other in front and behind.

Molecular recognition and ethylene (PDF p.253): biological molecules act by fitting receptors with complementary 3-D shapes. Tomatoes ripen faster in a bag because they give off ethylene and have receptors that respond to it.

Acrolein, a component of barbecue smoke and a possible carcinogen, has three sp2 carbons, so “all the atoms in a molecule of acrolein could be in the same plane” (PDF p.253–254).

Benzene and conjugation (PDF p.254): six sp2 carbons fit a flat ring, because sp2 angles “are the same as the 120° inside angles of a regular hexagon.” Alternating single and double bonds between atoms with unhybridized p orbitals is conjugation, and benzene's π electrons are “delocalized over all six carbon atoms, forming two clouds of electrons above and below the hexagonal rings.”

Delocalization and stability (PDF p.255): spreading electrons out lowers their mutual repulsion and the molecule's potential energy, and “the greater the degree of delocalization, the greater the stability.” An aromatic compound is “a cyclic, planar compound with delocalized π electrons above and below the plane of the molecule.” The professor states that delocalized molecules are more stable but calls the reason “beyond the scope of this class” (Day 9 p.10).

Polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (Fig. 5.36, PDF p.255): fused benzene rings, the four molecules on Day 11 p.26. They form in fires and are found in cigarette smoke; because they're flat, they can slide between the strands of DNA's double helix (intercalation), “one step in the process by which PAHs may induce cancer.”

Common mistakes

  • Counting both lines of a double bond as σ bonds (ethylene has 5 σ, not 6).
  • Forgetting the C–H bonds when counting σ bonds.
  • Calling a carbon sp3 because it has four bond lines, when one of them is part of a double bond.
  • Treating the π bond's two lobes as two bonds.
  • Putting a lone pair in the unhybridized p orbital: diazene's lone pairs are in sp2 hybrids.
