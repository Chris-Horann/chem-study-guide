Learn

When two or more valid Lewis structures “can be interconverted by just moving electrons, we say they are ‘in resonance’” (Day 9 p.8). Ozone is the lecture's example, and its real bonds match neither drawing: “neither structure alone is correct.” The molecule is “ALWAYS somewhere in between. An average of the two” (Day 9 p.9).

What we observe

Both O–O bonds in ozone are 128 pm long. A typical O–O bond is 148 pm and a typical O=O bond 121 pm: “The bonds in ozone are somewhere in between” (Day 9 p.7). Ozone itself is an allotrope of oxygen, made from O2 by lightning (Day 8 p.27).

What we write

↔

The two structures joined by the resonance arrow ↔ (Day 9 p.9). They are “equivalent. That does not mean they are the same” (Day 9 p.8).

What the electrons are doing

Turning one drawing into the other moves only electrons, never atoms: the red curved arrows on Day 9 p.9 show which pairs shift. In the real molecule those electrons are “delocalized,” spread over both bonds at once (Day 9 p.10).

Source: Day 9 p.6–13, with Day 8 p.26–30.

Understand

From the Day 8 answer to the hybrid (Day 9 p.6–10)

  1. Two answers. The five steps give O=O–O and O–O=O: the Day 8 practice exercise, repeated (p.6).
  2. “Which of these structures is correct? Interestingly, the answer turns out to be ‘neither.’” Both bonds are 128 pm, between O–O (148 pm, drawn as H–O–O–H) and O=O (121 pm, drawn as O2) (p.7).
  3. Equivalent, not the same. “We can transform one of these structures into the other by only moving electrons,” so they are “in resonance,” and each is a “resonance structure” (p.8).
  4. Curved arrows and ↔. Red curved arrows show which pairs move, with a plain → to the result; ↔ joins the resonance structures. “The molecule is NOT ‘changing’ back and forth between these two structures” (p.9).
  5. The resonance hybrid. “An even better way to draw this structure is as the ‘resonance hybrid’, where we use dashed lines to indicate partial bonds” (p.10).

Benzene (Day 9 p.12–13)

Benzene's formula, C6H6, was known in the mid-1800s, “but with the chemistry we knew at the time, we couldn't draw any structures that made any sense.” Kekulé's ring of alternating single and double bonds (around 1865, “inspired by a dream he had of a snake devouring its own tail”) was “nearly correct” (Day 9 p.12). His two structures are in resonance:

“But benzene doesn't behave as though it has double bonds – its chemistry is fundamentally different than any alkenes. It behaves, instead, as though it is a resonance hybrid” (Day 9 p.13). The slide draws that hybrid two ways: every ring bond as a solid line plus a dashed line, or a hexagon with a circle inside.

Day 9's Representation Matters slide (history) honors Kathleen Lonsdale, whose crystallography showed in 1929 that the C–C bonds of the benzene ring are all the same length, between a single and a double bond: “a hugely important fact that everyone who takes General Chemistry learns, but nobody ever tells you HOW we know that” (Day 9 p.2).

The 1929 structure was of hexamethylbenzene, a benzene ring carrying six CH3 groups; the slide says “benzene.”

Recognize it

  • Two valid structures with the same skeleton that differ only in where a double bond or lone pair sits → resonance structures; join them with ↔.
  • Same atoms in the same places, only electrons moved → in resonance. An atom moved → a different molecule.
  • A measured bond length between the single and double values, like ozone's 128 pm → the bond is an average: resonance.
  • Equivalent structures (ozone's two) need no ranking. Nonequivalent ones (N2O's three) are ranked with formal charge (§4.7).

Common mistakes

  • Treating one resonance structure as the real molecule, with one 121 pm bond and one 148 pm bond.
  • Thinking the molecule flips between the structures: it's “NOT ‘changing’ back and forth” (Day 9 p.9).
  • Moving atoms, not just electrons, between “resonance” drawings.
  • Confusing ↔ (resonance) with ⇌ (equilibrium).
