Learn

Sometimes the five steps give several valid structures that are “not equivalent.” Which is right? “Well, again, the answer is ‘none of them’… But one of them is better than the other two, and closer to the real structure” (Day 9 p.21). To choose, the professor introduces formal charge, “a comparison of how many electrons the atoms have in the compound to how many they had as free atoms” (Day 9 p.22).

What we observe

Dinitrogen monoxide, N2O, is one substance with one real structure. The practice exercise on Day 9 p.19 counts its valence electrons in a table: N 2 × 5 = 10, O 1 × 6 = 6, “TOTAL: 16,” with the skeleton N–N–O.

What we write

The three structures the five steps allow (Day 9 p.20): each uses all 16 electrons and gives every atom an octet.

What the electrons are doing

In this bookkeeping each atom “owns” its lone pairs and half of every bond it forms. An atom assigned more electrons than it brought as a free atom gets a negative formal charge; fewer, a positive one.

Source: Day 9 p.19–26.

Understand

FC = (valence e−) − [(unshared e−) + ½(e− in bonding pairs)]

“For each atom” (Day 9 p.22): 1. Determine the number of valence electrons in the free atom. 2. Count the number of electrons in lone pairs on the atom in the structure. 3. Count the number of electrons in bonds to the atom and divide that number by 2. 4. Sum the results of 2 and 3 and subtract from the number determined in step 1.

valence e−
the free atom's valence electrons: the dots in its Lewis symbol (blue on the slide)
unshared e−
electrons in the atom's lone pairs in this structure, 2 per pair, plus any single unpaired electron (red)
e− in bonding pairs
all the electrons in bonds to the atom, 2 per bond, so a double bond counts 4 (green)

Worked: structure A (:N≡N–O with three lone pairs on O). End N: 5 − (2 + ½ × 6) = 0. Central N: 5 − (0 + ½ × 8) = +1. O: 6 − (6 + ½ × 2) = −1. Sum 0, as it must be for a molecule.

Choosing among structures (Day 9 p.23)

“When choosing between multiple Lewis structures:”

  1. “The best structure is the one in which the formal charge on each atom is zero.”
  2. “If no such structure can be drawn, the best structure is the one where most of the atoms have formal charges equal to zero or as close to zero as possible.”
  3. “Any negative formal charges should be on the atom(s) of the more/most electronegative element.”
  4. “If you've done it right, the sum of the formal charges will be the charge on the molecule/ion.”

Connected lecture ideas

Rule 3 uses electronegativity (Day 9 p.17–18, §4.2): with N 3.0 and O 3.5, a −1 belongs on O before N. Formal charges reappear in red on the octet exceptions, NO2 and SO42− (Day 9 p.28, p.30, §4.8), where expanding sulfur's octet brings them “closer to zero” (Day 9 p.29).

The Day 9 p.24 table calls N2O's structures “resonance structures” although Day 9 p.21 calls them “not equivalent.” By the p.8 definition, interconverted “by just moving electrons,” they are in resonance; formal charge decides which contributes most.

Textbook preview (§4.7, PDF p.208–212, printed 174–178)

“Not a real charge.” “A formal charge (FC) is not a real charge, but rather an accounting system” for the electrons formally assigned to each atom (PDF p.209).

The same rules, worded a little differently. The textbook lists three criteria (PDF p.210). Its rule 2 adds “or if the structure is that of a polyatomic ion,” and it states the sum check (the slide's rule 4) in its text instead of its list.

A shortcut. With octets, an atom that forms as many bonds as its bonding capacity (Day 8 p.18) has FC 0; one more bond gives +1; one fewer gives −1 (PDF p.211).

CO2. O=C=O, with every formal charge 0, beats the two O≡C–O structures (Sample Ex. 4.16, PDF p.211–212).

A reality check. For N2O the textbook adds that the measured N–N bond length lies between a triple and a double bond, so the real molecule lies between two of the structures: “Just because a resonance structure scores the best in an FC analysis does not mean that it matches experimental data” (PDF p.210–211).

Recognize it

  • More than one valid structure, and they aren't equivalent → compute formal charges and apply the rules.
  • “Formal charge on an atom” → the four steps, one atom at a time.
  • Formal charges that don't add up to the overall charge → a counting error (rule 4).
  • A structure that fails the electron count → reject it before computing anything.

Common mistakes

  • Counting all the bonding electrons instead of half (that's the octet count).
  • Forgetting lone-pair electrons, or counting pairs instead of electrons.
  • Treating formal charge as a real charge, or assigning it from electronegativity.
  • Skipping rule 4's check that the formal charges add up to the overall charge.
