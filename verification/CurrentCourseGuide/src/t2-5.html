Learn

Atoms are too small to count one by one, so chemists count them by the mole: exactly 6.02214076 × 1023 particles. The number is chosen so that a mole of any substance has a mass in grams equal to its formula mass in amu. The mole is the bridge between the balance and the atom.

What we observe

Weigh out 196.97 g of gold, 12.011 g of carbon, or 18.015 g of water: each pile contains the same number of units, one mole.

What we write

mass ÷ ℳ (g/mol) = moles; moles × NA = particles (NA = 6.022 × 1023 mol−1) (textbook Fig. 2.18).

What the particles are doing

One gold atom has a mass of 196.97 amu = 3.2708 × 10−22 g, far too small to weigh. NA of them weigh 196.97 g.

Source: textbook §2.5, PDF p.98–104 (printed 64–70).

Understand

n = m / ℳ; N = n × NA

m
mass (g)
ℳ
molar mass (g/mol), the sum of the element molar masses in the formula
n
amount (mol)
N
number of particles: atoms, molecules, or formula units

Why ℳ in g/mol equals the mass in amu 1 amu × 6.022 × 1023 ≈ 1 g, so NA particles of 196.97 amu each weigh 196.97 g.

Subscripts are mole ratios 1 mol C6H12O6 contains 6 mol C, 12 mol H, and 6 mol O atoms.

Always pass through moles grams ↔ moles ↔ particles; there's no direct grams ↔ particles step.

Recognize it

  • Grams given, a count of particles asked (or the reverse) → through moles.
  • The mass of one molecule → ℳ ÷ NA.
  • Volume given → density first, then moles.

Common mistakes

  • Multiplying grams by NA.
  • Multiplying by ℳ when you should divide (check the units).
  • Forgetting the subscripts when counting atoms within molecules.
