## Convention differences

Where the textbook's notation, terminology, or method differs from the
professor's. Mirror each entry in `COURSE.md` → Discrepancies.

- **Atomic mass unit**: professor "amu" = dalton (Day 2 p.21–23); textbook "unified atomic mass unit (u)" = dalton (TB PDF p.86). Guides use amu and mention u.
- **Photoelectric symbols**: professor "threshold energy, φ (also called the 'work function')", KE = hν − φ = hν − hν₀ (Day 3 p.19); textbook "work function (Φ)", Φ = hν − KE_electron, "threshold frequency ν₀" (TB PDF p.127–128).
- **Balmer variable**: professor n (integer ≥ 3) (Day 3 p.21); textbook m (integer > 2) (TB PDF p.129).
- **Ionization equation**: professor M (g) + hν → M⁺ (g) + e⁻ (Day 7 p.9); textbook Mg → Mg⁺ + 1 e⁻, no photon (TB PDF p.159).
- **Configuration order**: professor writes both filling order (Mo [Kr]5s²4d⁴, Day 7 p.6) and n order (Ni [Ar]3d⁸4s², Day 6 p.21); textbook writes n order ([Ar]3d¹4s², [Ar]3d⁵4s¹, TB PDF p.150–151).
- **Bond types**: professor covalent "between non-metals", metallic electrons "highly mobile" (Day 7 p.14); textbook Table 4.1 "Nonmetals and metalloids", "Delocalized" (TB PDF p.184).
- **Section numbering (edition)**: professor Ch. 4 = 4.2 "Electronegativity and Bonding", 4.5 Lengths and Strengths, 4.6 Resonance (Day 7 p.12); supplied book 4.2 "Electronegativity, Unequal Sharing, and Polar Bonds", 4.5 Resonance, 4.6 Lengths and Strengths (TB PDF p.8).
- **Table 4.2**: slide includes CaS −3093 kJ/mol (Day 7 p.17); supplied book's Table 4.2 has no CaS row (TB PDF p.184).
- **Neutron mass (kg)**: slide 1.67483 × 10⁻²⁷ (Day 2 p.22); textbook 1.67493 × 10⁻²⁷ (TB PDF p.87), consistent with 1.00866 u. Slide typo.
- **Sig-fig rounding ties** (textbook only): round a trailing 5 to the nearest even digit (76.45 → 76.4; 76.55 → 76.6), and round only at the end of a calculation (TB PDF p.57–58, printed 23–24). The lecture states no rounding policy.
- **Electronegativity of Li**: Fig. 4.5 prints 1.1 (TB PDF p.187); many other tables list 1.0. Guides use the textbook value and avoid problems that hinge on it.
- **Roman numerals**: slide "copper (II) oxide" with a space (Day 8 p.7); textbook "copper(II) oxide" (TB PDF p.191). Same meaning; guides can show the textbook spacing and accept both.
- **Polyatomic ions, memorization**: professor: "You will be provided with a table like this on the exams, so you don't need to memorize them" (Day 8 p.8); textbook: "you need to memorize the formulas, charges, and names of the common polyatomic ions" (TB PDF p.192, printed 158). The professor's step 5, "Use any leftover electrons to complete the octet on the central atom" (Day 8 p.21), leaves out the textbook's instruction for a central atom still short of an octet: "create additional bonds to it by converting one or more lone pairs of electrons on the outer atoms into bonding pairs" (TB PDF p.197). The professor's C₂H₂ and O₃ examples do exactly that (Day 8 p.25, p.30). The textbook's electronegativity tie-breaker and its polyatomic-ion charge count aren't on the slides.

**Ch. 1 Matter and Energy: An Atomic Perspective (printed 2–45, PDF 36–79)**
- §1.1 (4–7, PDF 38–41): scientific methods: observation → hypothesis → testing → theory; law vs. theory (laws describe, theories explain) (Fig. 1.3, PDF 40); classification-of-matter flowchart (Fig. 1.2, PDF 39). The laws and Dalton are lecture content (Day 1).
- §1.2 COAST (7–8, PDF 41–42): Collect and Organize, Analyze (work backward, check units, estimate), Solve, Think About It (reasonable? units? sig figs?). "A framework … not a recipe."
- §1.3 (8–13, PDF 42–47): matter, mass; pure substances (elements, compounds) vs. mixtures (homogeneous = solutions; heterogeneous; immiscible liquids); physical vs. chemical processes and properties; intensive vs. extensive properties; density d = m/V (Eq. 1.1); particles may be atoms, molecules, or ions. Separating mixtures (11–13): distillation (volatility, which falls as interparticle attraction rises), filtration (particle size vs. pore size), chromatography (stationary vs. mobile phase; stronger interaction with the stationary phase moves more slowly).
- §1.4 (13–16, PDF 47–50): solid (definite shape and volume), liquid (definite volume), gas/vapor (neither; compressible, mostly empty space); particle views of ice, water, vapor (Fig. 1.9); phase changes (Fig. 1.10): melting, vaporization, sublimation absorb energy; freezing, condensation, deposition release energy.
- §1.5 (16–17, PDF 50–51): energy = capacity to do work; w = F × d (Eq. 1.2); potential energy (position or composition; chemical energy); kinetic energy KE = ½mu² (Eq. 1.3); doubling the speed quadruples KE; heat = energy transfer due to a temperature difference; particle KE rises solid < liquid < gas. (The law of conservation of energy is lecture content, Day 1 p.15.)
- §1.6 (17–19, PDF 51–53): molecules; diatomic elements H₂, N₂, O₂, F₂, Cl₂, Br₂, I₂; molecular formula; structural formula; condensed structural formula; ball-and-stick and space-filling models (acetone C₃H₆O, acetic acid C₂H₄O₂; Fig. 1.15); ionic compounds are not molecules; empirical formula = simplest whole-number ratio (NaCl).
- §1.7 (19–26, PDF 53–60): meter history; SI base units (Table 1.2: kg, m, K, s, A, mol); prefixes (Table 1.1, zetta 10²¹ … zepto 10⁻²¹); joule = 1 kg·(m/s)²; exact (counted or defined) vs. uncertain values; precision (repeatability) vs. accuracy (closeness to the true value; calibration) (Fig. 1.22 darts); significant figures = certain digits plus one estimated digit; zero rules (leading never significant; trailing after a decimal point always; trailing without a decimal point ambiguous, so use scientific notation; captive always); round only at the end; round-half-to-even tie rule; weak-link principle: × and ÷ keep the fewest sig figs, + and − keep the fewest decimal places; exact numbers don't limit.
- §1.8 (26–31, PDF 60–65): Gimli Glider (7700 L × 0.80 kg/L; the 1.77 lb/L error); conversion factors are ratios equal to 1 (Eq. 1.4); Table 1.3 equivalencies (1 kg = 2.205 lb; 1 in = 2.54 cm exactly; 1 m³ = 1000 L exactly; 1 L = 0.2642 gal = 1.057 qt; T(K) = T(°C) + 273.15; T(°C) = (5/9)[T(°F) − 32]); estimate first; cube length factors for volumes; exact factors don't limit sig figs; KE of a served tennis ball (km/h → m/s, g → kg).
- §1.9 (31–37, PDF 65–71): control samples; mean x̄ = Σxᵢ/n (Eq. 1.5); standard deviation s = √[Σ(xᵢ − x̄)²/(n − 1)] (Eq. 1.6); report x̄ ± s; confidence interval μ = x̄ ± ts/√n (Eq. 1.7) with Table 1.5 (t at 90/95/99% for n − 1 = 3, 4, 5, 10, 20, ∞; the 95% values are 3.182, 2.776, 2.571, 2.228, 2.086, 1.960); normal distribution, 68% within 1 s; outliers and Grubbs' test Z = |xᵢ − x̄|/s (Eq. 1.8) vs. Table 1.7 (95% column for n = 3–12: 1.155, 1.481, 1.715, 1.887, 2.020, 2.126, 2.215, 2.290, 2.355, 2.412); discarding data without a valid reason is unethical; Grubbs' test is used only once per data set.

**Ch. 2 Atoms, Ions, and Molecules (printed 46–83, PDF 80–117)**
- §2.1 Radioactivity (50–52, PDF 84–86): Becquerel (1896), the Curies, Rutherford; radioactivity = spontaneous emission of high-energy radiation and particles; β particles = high-energy electrons (1−); α particles = ⁴He nuclei (2+); they bend in opposite directions in a magnetic field; Geiger and Marsden saw "about 1 in every 8000" α particles deflected by an average of 90° (PDF 85). The nuclear atom and Chadwick (1932): PDF 86.
- §2.2 (53–56, PDF 87–90): Aston's positive-ray analyzer (Ne-20 ≈ 90%, Ne-22 ≈ 10%); isotope; nuclide; atomic number Z; mass number A; nucleon; symbol ᴬ_ZX (Z may be omitted: ᴬX; names such as neon-20 or Ne-20); ions ᴬX^Q, with electrons = Z − Q; particle symbols ¹₀n, ¹₁p, ⁰₋₁e (the subscript is the relative charge).
- §2.3 (56–60, PDF 90–94): Mendeleev (1872: atomic-mass order, gaps, predictions); modern table in Z order, 7 periods and 18 groups; metals, nonmetals, metalloids (properties); main-group (representative) elements = groups 1, 2, 13–18; transition metals = groups 3–12; lanthanides (Z = 58–71) and actinides (90–103); every isotope above Z = 83 is radioactive; group names: alkali metals (group 1 except H), alkaline earth metals (2), chalcogens (16), halogens (17), noble gases (18); common monatomic ion charges (Fig. 2.11; Table 2.2: groups 1 +1, 2 +2, 3 +3, 4 +4, 11 +1, 12 +2, 13 +3, 15 −3, 16 −2, 17 −1); cation and anion; 1 : 1 and 1 : 2 ionic formulas (NaCl, CaCl₂).
- §2.4 (60–64, PDF 94–98): average atomic mass as a weighted average, m_X = a₁m₁ + a₂m₂ + … (Eq. 2.3), with abundances as decimals and one extra digit carried (Ne: 20.1799 u); natural abundances vary with the source (a seawater Cl sample gave 35.455 u); two-isotope abundance problems (x and 1 − x); molecular mass (SO₂ 64.063 u); formula unit and formula mass for ionic compounds (CaCl₂ 110.984 u); electron masses are negligible for ions.
- §2.5 (64–70, PDF 98–104): 1 u = 1.66054 × 10⁻²⁷ kg; the mole (exactly 6.02214076 × 10²³ entities; calculations use 6.022 × 10²³ mol⁻¹); particles ↔ moles by ÷ or × N_A (Fig. 2.14); molar mass ℳ (g/mol) is numerically equal to the atomic, molecular, or formula mass in u; molar masses of compounds from element molar masses; the mass ↔ moles ↔ particles map (Fig. 2.18); the "chemist's dozen."

- §4.7 Formal Charge (174–178, PDF 208–212):
  - N₂O (16 e⁻) has three non-equivalent structures: A N≡N–O (0, +1, −1), B N=N=O (−1, +1, 0), C N–N≡O (−2, +1, +1).
  - Eq. 4.2: FC = valence e⁻ − [lone-pair e⁻ + ½ shared e⁻]. FCs sum to 0 for a molecule, or to the ion's charge.
  - Three criteria: all zero; otherwise most atoms zero or nearest zero; any negative FC on the more electronegative atom. A wins; the real N–N bond lies between A and B.
  - Concept Test: S with 3 lone pairs + 1 bond has FC −1.
  - Shortcut: bonds = bonding capacity → FC 0; one more bond → +1; one fewer → −1.
  - Sample Ex. 4.16: CO₂, where O=C=O (all 0) beats the O≡C–O forms.
- §4.8 Exceptions (178–183, PDF 212–217):
  - Electron-deficient Be (4 e⁻) and B, Al (6 e⁻).
  - Odd-electron molecules NO (11 e⁻) and NO₂ (17 e⁻) are free radicals. N keeps the incomplete octet and O the complete one; NO₂'s FCs are 0/+1/−1, and NO₂ dimerizes to N₂O₄.
  - Expanded octets for Z > 12: PCl₅ (10 e⁻ on P), SF₆ (12 e⁻ on S). "d orbitals contribute little". This happens with F, O, Cl, or when it brings FCs closer to zero.
  - SO₄²⁻ drawn with all octets has FC S +2, O −1; with two S=O, S is 0. H₂SO₄ is all zero. Experiment says the real bonding averages both kinds of structure.
  - Sample Ex. 4.18: PO₄³⁻ with one P=O gives FC P 0 and resonance over four O.
- §4.9 Vibrating Bonds and the Greenhouse Effect (183–185, PDF 217–219):
  - Bonds vibrate like springs. A polar bond's vibration produces an oscillating field that can absorb IR at the same frequency.
  - CO₂: the symmetric stretch is IR-inactive; the asymmetric stretch and the bend are IR-active (Fig. 4.13). CO₂ is a potent greenhouse gas; Earth's surface (287 K) emits IR.
  - Concept Test: N≡N and O=O stretches are IR-inactive, since the bonds are nonpolar.
  - Sample Ex. 4.19: PDB (para-dichlorobenzene), 42 e⁻, resonance; the C–C bond order 1.5 gives about 144 pm and 481 kJ/mol; C–Cl is polar (Δχ 0.5).
- Summary and Problem-Solving Summary (185–186, PDF 219–220): LO1–LO8. Naming binary molecular compounds: first write the element that is farther left, or lower in its group.
