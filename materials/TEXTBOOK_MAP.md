  - The test for resonance: one atom with single and double bonds to atoms of the same element. Benzene C₆H₆ and its circle notation (Fig. 4.10).
- §4.6 Lengths and Strengths (172–174, PDF 206–208):
  - Bond order = number of shared pairs; length falls and energy rises as bond order increases.
  - Fig. 4.12: C–H 110/111 pm in CH₄/CH₂O; C=O 121–123 pm; C≡O 113 pm.
  - Table 4.6 (PDF 207): average lengths (pm) and energies (kJ/mol) of 35 bonds, read off the render; footnote: C=O in CO₂ 799 kJ/mol (printed "C—O").
  - Sample Ex. 4.15: CO₃²⁻, bond order 4/3 = 1.33, length between 143 and 123 pm (measured 129 pm).
  - Bond energy = energy to break one mole of bonds in the gas phase, always positive (H–H 436 = the depth of Fig. 4.2); C=O 799 in CO₂ vs. 743 kJ/mol in CH₂O.
- §4.7 Formal Charge (174–178, PDF 208–212):
  - N₂O (16 e⁻) has three non-equivalent structures: A N≡N–O (0, +1, −1), B N=N=O (−1, +1, 0), C N–N≡O (−2, +1, +1).
  - Eq. 4.2: FC = valence e⁻ − [lone-pair e⁻ + ½ shared e⁻]. FCs sum to 0 for a molecule, or to the ion's charge.
  - Three criteria: all zero; otherwise most atoms zero or nearest zero; any negative FC on the more electronegative atom. A wins; the real N–N bond lies between A and B.
  - Concept Test: S with 3 lone pairs + 1 bond has FC −1.
  - Shortcut: bonds = bonding capacity → FC 0; one more bond → +1; one fewer → −1.
  - Sample Ex. 4.16: CO₂, where O=C=O (all 0) beats the O≡C–O forms.
- §4.8 Exceptions (178–183, PDF 212–217):
  - Electron-deficient Be (4 e⁻) and B, Al (6 e⁻).
  - Odd-electron molecules NO (11 e⁻) and NO₂ (17 e⁻) are free radicals. N keeps the incomplete octet and O the complete one; NO₂'s FCs are 0/+1/−1, and NO₂ dimerizes to N₂O₄.
  - Expanded octets for Z > 12: PCl₅ (10 e⁻ on P), SF₆ (12 e⁻ on S). "d orbitals contribute little". This happens with F, O, Cl, or when it brings FCs closer to zero.
  - SO₄²⁻ drawn with all octets has FC S +2, O −1; with two S=O, S is 0. H₂SO₄ is all zero. Experiment says the real bonding averages both kinds of structure.
  - Sample Ex. 4.18: PO₄³⁻ with one P=O gives FC P 0 and resonance over four O.
- §4.9 Vibrating Bonds and the Greenhouse Effect (183–185, PDF 217–219):
  - Bonds vibrate like springs. A polar bond's vibration produces an oscillating field that can absorb IR at the same frequency.
  - CO₂: the symmetric stretch is IR-inactive; the asymmetric stretch and the bend are IR-active (Fig. 4.13). CO₂ is a potent greenhouse gas; Earth's surface (287 K) emits IR.
  - Concept Test: N≡N and O=O stretches are IR-inactive, since the bonds are nonpolar.
  - Sample Ex. 4.19: PDB (para-dichlorobenzene), 42 e⁻, resonance; the C–C bond order 1.5 gives about 144 pm and 481 kJ/mol; C–Cl is polar (Δχ 0.5).
- Summary and Problem-Solving Summary (185–186, PDF 219–220): LO1–LO8. Naming binary molecular compounds: first write the element that is farther left, or lower in its group.

## Textbook notes: Ch. 18 §18.4–18.5 (read 2026-10-06; Day 12 p.14 bolds both)

- §18.4 Metallic Bonds and Conduction Bands (884–886, PDF 918–920). Malleability ("the ability to be shaped") and ductility ("the ability to be drawn out") partly because "the bonds between atoms in a solid are weak". VB view: Na₂ would have one covalent 3s–3s bond (Fig. 18.23a), but solid Na is body-centered cubic, each atom bonded to eight others (Fig. 18.23b), so each shares its one 3s electron with eight neighbors; "this dispersion of bonding electrons weakens the Na–Na bond", and sodium can be cut with a knife (Fig. 18.24). The Ch. 4 sea of electrons "certainly fits", but band theory, "an extension of molecular orbital theory", explains better. MO view (Fig. 18.25): two 3s orbitals → two MOs "above and below the initial value"; Na₄ → four MOs, the lower filled; Na_N → "an equally enormous number of molecular orbitals", "a continuous band of energies with no gap between the occupied lower half and the empty upper half", the valence band; electrons move easily into the empty upper portion and flow through the solid. The 3s band broadens and overlaps the empty 3p band. Zn, [Ar]3d¹⁰4s²: a filled 4s valence band; the empty 4p conduction band "is broad enough to overlap the valence band" (Fig. 18.26); "Either model supports the same conclusion: the valence electrons in zinc metal are mobile". Conductor defined. Margin terms: band theory, valence band, conduction band, conductor. Concept Test: magnesium.
- §18.5 Semiconductors (886–887, PDF 920–921). Metalloids, a "staircase" with metals' physical and nonmetals' chemical properties, conduct better than nonmetals but worse than metals: their bands "do not overlap but instead are separated by an energy gap". Si: band gap (E_g) 106 kJ/mol at 25 °C (Fig. 18.27a); "only a few valence-band electrons in Si have enough energy to move to the conduction band", so Si is a semiconductor. Doping: replace some Si with atoms "having a similar atomic radius but a different number of valence electrons" (dopant); doped semiconductors "represent substitutional alloys". P (group 15, one more valence electron; the Lewis symbols of Si and P in Fig. 18.27): a donor level "only about 4 kJ/mol below the Si conduction band" → n-type ("the dopant donates negative charges (electrons)"). Ga (group 13, one fewer): an acceptor level "about 7 kJ/mol above the Si valence band"; valence electrons move into it, "leaving behind positively charged “holes”" (⊕), and electrons migrating between holes enhance conductivity → p-type. Devices combine n- and p-type. NaCl, an insulator, "has an enormous band gap (6.8 × 10⁵ kJ/mol)" [suspect value, see Convention differences]; Zn has "essentially no band gap". Margin terms: band gap, semiconductor, n-type ("electron-rich dopant"), p-type ("electron-poor dopant"), insulator. Then (PDF 921, still §18.5): group 13–15 alloys (GaAs; (3 + 5)/2 = 4 valence electrons per atom; larger gaps; IR emission at λ = 874 nm, AlGaAs₂ at 620 nm; LEDs, blue from InₓGa₁₋ₓN and GaN; white light from red + green + blue); CdS and CdSe. Concept Test: GaAs doped with Se or Sn.
